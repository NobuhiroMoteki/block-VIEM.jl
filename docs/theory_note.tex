\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%======================================================================

\texttt{block-VIEM.jl} is a Julia implementation of the volume integral
equation method (VIEM) for electromagnetic scattering by arbitrarily
shaped, high-contrast dielectric particles. It is designed as a
higher-accuracy successor to the discrete dipole approximation (DDA)
code \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}~\cite{Moteki2024}, targeting scattering
problems where DDA does not converge or provides insufficient accuracy
--- for example, particles with high refractive-index contrast (e.g.\
plasmonic metals), extreme aspect ratios, or surface roughness features
smaller than the DDA lattice spacing.

The key features of \texttt{block-VIEM.jl} are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Higher accuracy per degree of freedom than DDA.}
        Tetrahedral mesh discretization with SWG (half-SWG) basis
        functions~\cite{SWG1984} and Duffy-transform singular
        integration achieves 10--12$\times$ better accuracy per DOF
        than cubic-lattice DDA on equivalent problems.
  \item \textbf{AIM/FFT-accelerated block-Krylov solver.}
        The Adaptive Integral Method (AIM)~\cite{Bleszynski1996}
        computes matrix--vector products in $O(N \log N)$ via FFT.
        Block BiCGSTAB and Block GMRES solve for all particle
        orientations simultaneously.
  \item \textbf{Multi-orientation batch solve with spheroid mode.}
        Deterministic Euler angle grids on SO(3) identical to
        \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}. For spheroids, only $N_\beta$
        orientations are solved; the full grid is filled analytically.
  \item \textbf{Birefringent (anisotropic) materials.}
        Diagonal permittivity tensor
        $\epsilon_p = \mathrm{diag}(\epsilon_x, \epsilon_y, \epsilon_z)$.
  \item \textbf{Two particle-shape families with automatic mesh generation.}
        Gaussian Random Ellipsoid (GRE, parametric irregular shapes
        matching \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}) and sphere aggregates
        (chains, planar arrays, FCC/BCC/HCP clusters, PTSA fractal-like
        aggregates).  Both are meshed with adaptive tetrahedral
        resolution via Gmsh and a volume-preserving final rescale
        (specified in
        \texttt{docs/descriptions\_particle\_shape\_model.md}).
  \item \textbf{CAS-v2 observables and block-DDA\_Py-compatible I/O.}
        Forward/backward complex scattering amplitudes (PCAS, OCBS),
        cross sections, and HDF5 parameter-sweep output in the same
        schema as \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}.
\end{itemize}

The two codes share the same physical input conventions, HDF5 output
schema, and CAS-v2 observable definitions, so downstream analysis
pipelines work with either without modification.

\paragraph{Scope of this note.}
This document describes the theoretical background and algorithms
implemented in \texttt{block-VIEM.jl}, covering:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the EFVIE-D formulation with SWG and half-SWG basis functions
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation}),
  \item weakening the $1/R^3$ singularity to $1/R$ via integration by
        parts, including the boundary-face surface-correction terms
        $K^{B}$, $K^{C}$, $K^{D}$ required to restore charge neutrality
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:weakening}),
  \item evaluation of the remaining $1/R$ singular and near-singular
        volume integrals via the generalized Duffy transform
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:duffy}),
  \item FFT-accelerated matrix--vector products through the Adaptive
        Integral Method (AIM) with moment matching and precorrection
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim}),
  \item the direct and Krylov iterative solvers and the
        multi-orientation batch solve
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:solver}),
  \item the CAS-v2 compatible scattering observables computed from the
        solution (Sec.~\ref{sec:observables}),
  \item validation against Mie theory and the oblate-spheroid symmetry
        identity (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}),
  \item the HDF5 output format for spheroid parameter sweeps, bit
        compatible with the \texttt{block-DDA\_Py} schema consumed by
        \texttt{build\_spheroid\_lut.py}
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:spheroid-sweep-io}),
  \item the shifted block GMRES that solves a sweep of the particle
        refractive index with one Krylov process
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:shifted-gmres}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Notation and conventions.}
Throughout this note the electromagnetic equations are written in the
physics (BH83~\cite{Bohren1983}) time convention $\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t}$,
so that an outgoing scalar Helmholtz Green's function carries the factor
$\mathrm{e}^{+ik_0 R}/(4\pi R)$. Absorbing materials have
$\Im(\epsilon_p) > 0$. Lengths are expressed in micrometres
(\si{\micro\metre}). Vectors are set in bold italic
($\bm{E}$, $\bm{k}$, $\bm{r}$), matrices and second-rank tensors in
upright bold ($\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{G}$), and unit vectors carry a hat
($\hat{\bm{e}}$, $\hat{\bm{n}}$).

\section{VIEM Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{EFVIE-D strong form}
\label{sec:efvie-d}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a non-magnetic ($\mu = \mu_0$) inhomogeneous dielectric particle
occupying the volume $\Omega$ with complex permittivity
$\epsilon_p(\bm{r})$, embedded in a homogeneous background of real
permittivity $\epsilon_{\text{bg}}$. The wavenumber in the background is
\begin{equation}
  k_0 = \omega\,\sqrt{\mu_0\,\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
      = \frac{2\pi\,m_m}{\lambda_0},
  \label{eq:wavenumber}
\end{equation}
where $m_m = \sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}/\epsilon_0}$ is the refractive
index of the background medium.

We use the electric flux volume integral equation in the
\emph{D-form}~\cite{SWG1984}: the unknown field is the electric flux
density $\bm{D}(\bm{r}) = \epsilon_p(\bm{r})\,\bm{E}(\bm{r})$, whose
normal component is continuous across material interfaces, so the
scheme places all dielectric jumps inside the basis functions and not
in the expansion coefficients. Define the dielectric contrast
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(\bm{r})
    = \frac{\epsilon_p(\bm{r}) - \epsilon_{\text{bg}}}{\epsilon_p(\bm{r})}.
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The equivalent volume polarisation current is
$\bm{J}_v = -i\omega\,\kappa\,\bm{D}$, and the EFVIE-D strong form
reads~\cite{SWG1984,VolakisSertel}
\begin{equation}
  \bm{E}_{\text{inc}}(\bm{r})
    = \frac{\bm{D}(\bm{r})}{\epsilon_p(\bm{r})}
      \;-\;
      \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
      \bigl(k_0^{2} + \grad\div\bigr)
      \int_{\Omega} \kappa(\bm{r}')\,\bm{D}(\bm{r}')\,
                    G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\;\dd^{3}r',
  \label{eq:efvie-strong}
\end{equation}
where $G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')$ is the free-space scalar Helmholtz Green's
function
\begin{equation}
  G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')
    = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{+i k_0 R}}{4\pi R},
  \qquad R = \abs{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'}.
  \label{eq:green-scalar}
\end{equation}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Incident plane wave}
\label{sec:incident-wave}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The incident field is a monochromatic plane wave
\begin{equation}
  \bm{E}_{\text{inc}}(\bm{r})
    = \bm{E}_0\,\exp\!\bigl(+i k_0\,\hat{\bm{k}}_{\text{inc}}\cdot\bm{r}\bigr),
  \label{eq:incident-wave}
\end{equation}
with $\hat{\bm{k}}_{\text{inc}}$ the unit propagation direction and
$\bm{E}_0$ a complex polarisation vector perpendicular to
$\hat{\bm{k}}_{\text{inc}}$. For CAS-v2 observables the incident
polarisation is left-handed circular (matching
\texttt{block-DDA\_Py}~\cite{Moteki2024}):
\begin{equation}
  \bm{E}_0
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{inc}}
      + \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{inc}},
  \label{eq:circular-pol}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{inc}},\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{inc}}$
are the polar and azimuthal unit vectors of the incident direction in
the particle frame. Particle orientation is parameterised by intrinsic
ZYZ Euler angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, following
\texttt{scipy.spatial.transform.Rotation} and \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}.
We denote by
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{R}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
  \;=\; \mathbf{R}_z(\alpha)\,\mathbf{R}_y(\beta)\,\mathbf{R}_z(\gamma)
  \label{eq:zyz-rotation}
\end{equation}
the active rotation that maps particle-frame coordinates of a physical
vector to its lab-frame coordinates; equivalently,
\eqref{eq:zyz-rotation} is the fixed-frame composition obtained by
applying the three extrinsic rotations
$\mathbf{R}_z(\gamma)$, $\mathbf{R}_y(\beta)$, $\mathbf{R}_z(\alpha)$
in that order about the lab axes. The laboratory-frame triad
$\{\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{inc},L},\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,L},\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,L}\}$
is therefore expressed in the particle frame by applying
$\mathbf{R}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)^{\top}$
($=\mathbf{R}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)^{-1}$ since $\mathbf{R}$ is
orthogonal).

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SWG and half-SWG basis functions}
\label{sec:swg}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The particle volume $\Omega$ is discretised into a conforming
tetrahedral mesh $\mathcal{T}_h$ with $N_{\text{tet}}$ elements.
The mesh is generated by Gmsh with a target characteristic edge
length~$\ell_c$, which controls the maximum edge length of every
tetrahedron; smaller $\ell_c$ yields a finer mesh with more elements
and degrees of freedom.
The two particle-shape families supported (Gaussian Random Ellipsoid
and sphere aggregates), the adaptive choice of~$\ell_c$, the
\texttt{neck\_ratio} neck-width convention, and the
volume-preserving final rescale are documented separately in
\texttt{docs/descriptions\_particle\_shape\_model.md}; the present
note proceeds from the assumption that $\mathcal{T}_h$ has been
generated and is conforming.
On each
internal face $S_n$ shared by two tetrahedra $T_n^{+}$ and $T_n^{-}$, an
SWG basis function~\cite[Eqs.~(10), (12)]{SWG1984} is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \bm{f}_n(\bm{r})
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle\frac{a_n}{3\,V_n^{+}}\,\bigl(\bm{r} - \bm{p}_n^{+}\bigr),
      & \bm{r}\in T_n^{+},\\[6pt]
    -\displaystyle\frac{a_n}{3\,V_n^{-}}\,\bigl(\bm{r} - \bm{p}_n^{-}\bigr),
      & \bm{r}\in T_n^{-},\\[6pt]
    \bm{0}, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:swg-def}
\end{equation}
where $a_n$ is the face area, $V_n^{\pm}$ the volumes of the two
supporting tetrahedra, and $\bm{p}_n^{\pm}$ the free vertex (the
vertex of $T_n^{\pm}$ not on $S_n$). The normalisation in
\eqref{eq:swg-def} enforces $\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}} = 1$ on
$S_n$ (oriented from $T_n^{+}$ to $T_n^{-}$) and zero on the other
five faces of $T_n^{+} \cup T_n^{-}$. The divergence is piecewise
constant,
\begin{equation}
  \div\bm{f}_n(\bm{r})
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle +\frac{a_n}{V_n^{+}}, & \bm{r}\in T_n^{+},\\[6pt]
    \displaystyle -\frac{a_n}{V_n^{-}}, & \bm{r}\in T_n^{-},\\[6pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:swg-div}
\end{equation}

A naive SWG expansion that uses only internal faces implicitly imposes
$\bm{D}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}} = 0$ on the particle boundary
$\partial\Omega$, leaving the surface polarisation charge unrepresented
and producing systematic cross-section errors of order
$10$--$30\%$ on Mie tests. To restore charge neutrality, the
original~\cite{SWG1984} formulation adds one half-SWG basis function per
boundary face. On a boundary face $S_n$ with a single supporting
tetrahedron $T_n^{+}$, the half-SWG function is the first branch of
\eqref{eq:swg-def} alone:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{f}_n^{\text{half}}(\bm{r})
    = \frac{a_n}{3\,V_n^{+}}\,\bigl(\bm{r} - \bm{p}_n^{+}\bigr),
  \qquad \bm{r} \in T_n^{+},
  \label{eq:half-swg}
\end{equation}
with $\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}} = 1$ on $S_n$ and zero on the other
three faces of $T_n^{+}$. For a half-SWG function the normal flux does
\emph{not} vanish on $\partial\Omega$, so it carries an induced surface
polarisation charge $\sigma = \kappa\,\bm{D}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}$ whose
integral over $S_n$ exactly cancels the bulk bound charge of the same
basis (Sec.~\ref{sec:charge-neutrality}). The electric flux density is
expanded as
\begin{equation}
  \bm{D}(\bm{r})
    \approx \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{dof}}} D_n\,\bm{f}_n(\bm{r}),
  \qquad
  N_{\text{dof}} = N_{\text{int. faces}} + N_{\text{bdy. faces}}.
  \label{eq:D-expansion}
\end{equation}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Galerkin weak form}
\label{sec:galerkin}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Testing \eqref{eq:efvie-strong} with a basis function $\bm{f}_m$ and
substituting \eqref{eq:D-expansion} yields the $N_{\text{dof}} \times
N_{\text{dof}}$ linear system
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{dof}}} Z_{mn}\,D_n = b_m,
  \qquad
  b_m = \int_{\Omega} \bm{f}_m(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{E}_{\text{inc}}(\bm{r})\,\dd^{3}r,
  \label{eq:linear-system}
\end{equation}
with the Galerkin impedance matrix element
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  Z_{mn}
    &= \int_{\Omega}
         \frac{\bm{f}_m(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{f}_n(\bm{r})}{\epsilon_p(\bm{r})}\,
         \dd^{3}r \\
    &\quad- \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
       \int_{\Omega}\bm{f}_m(\bm{r})\cdot
         \Bigl[
           \bigl(k_0^{2} + \grad\div\bigr)
           \int_{\Omega}\kappa(\bm{r}')\,\bm{f}_n(\bm{r}')\,
                        G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\dd^{3}r'
         \Bigr]\dd^{3}r.
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:Z-strong}
\end{equation}
For a homogeneous particle, $\kappa$ and $\epsilon_p$ are constants and
factor out of the inner integral, giving the compact decomposition
\begin{equation}
  Z_{mn} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_p}\,M_{mn}
           - \frac{\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}\,K_{mn},
  \label{eq:Z-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $M_{mn} = \int \bm{f}_m\!\cdot\!\bm{f}_n\,\dd^{3}r$ is the mass
matrix and $K_{mn}$ is the radiation kernel defined below.

%======================================================================
\section{Weakening the Singularity via Integration by Parts}
\label{sec:weakening}
%======================================================================

As written in \eqref{eq:Z-strong}, the radiation kernel
$(k_0^{2}+\grad\div) G$ contains a hyper-singular term $1/R^{3}$ coming
from $\grad\div G$. Moving the two derivatives off $G$ onto
$\bm{f}_m$ and $\bm{f}_n$ by successive integrations by parts reduces
the singularity to the weakly singular $1/R$ kernel that Duffy
quadrature can handle. Because SWG and half-SWG functions are only
piecewise smooth and only half-SWG functions have nonzero
$\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}$ on $\partial\Omega$, the by-parts
procedure generates three boundary-face contributions in addition to
the standard bulk term.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Bulk--bulk term $K^{A}$}
\label{sec:K-A}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Apply $\grad\div$ to the inner integral and move the inner divergence
onto $\bm{f}_n$ using the divergence theorem; do the same for the outer
divergence and $\bm{f}_m$. For internal SWG basis functions
(vanishing normal flux on $\partial\Omega$) the boundary terms are
identically zero and the resulting bulk kernel is
\begin{equation}
  K_{mn}^{A}
    = \int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
      \bigl[
        k_0^{2}\,\bm{f}_m(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{f}_n(\bm{r}')
        - (\div\bm{f}_m)(\bm{r})\,(\div'\bm{f}_n)(\bm{r}')
      \bigr]\,
      G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\dd^{3}r'\,\dd^{3}r.
  \label{eq:K-A}
\end{equation}
The integrand now contains only the weakly singular $1/R$ kernel and is
evaluated tetrahedron pair by tetrahedron pair.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Surface correction terms $K^{B}$, $K^{C}$, $K^{D}$}
\label{sec:K-BCD}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

When the source basis $\bm{f}_n$ is a half-SWG function attached to a
boundary face $S_n$, the inner integration by parts leaves a surface
term from the boundary $\partial\Omega$:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_n(\bm{r})
    \equiv \div\!\int_{\Omega} G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\bm{f}_n(\bm{r}')\,\dd^{3}r'
    = \int_{\Omega} G\,\div'\bm{f}_n\,\dd^{3}r'
      \;-\;\oint_{\partial\Omega}
          G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\bigl(\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}'\bigr)\,
          \dd^{2}r'.
  \label{eq:scalar-potential}
\end{equation}
Similarly, the outer integration by parts against $\bm{f}_m$ produces a
surface term if $\bm{f}_m$ is a half-SWG function on a boundary face
$S_m$. The complete kernel is
\begin{equation}
  K_{mn} = K_{mn}^{A} + K_{mn}^{B} + K_{mn}^{C} + K_{mn}^{D},
  \label{eq:K-full}
\end{equation}
with the three boundary contributions
\begin{align}
  K_{mn}^{B}
    &= \int_{\Omega}\!\oint_{\partial\Omega}
       (\div\bm{f}_m)(\bm{r})\,\bigl(\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}'\bigr)\,
       G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\dd^{2}r'\,\dd^{3}r,
  \label{eq:K-B} \\
  K_{mn}^{C}
    &= \oint_{\partial\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
       \bigl(\bm{f}_m\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}\bigr)\,(\div'\bm{f}_n)(\bm{r}')\,
       G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\dd^{3}r'\,\dd^{2}r,
  \label{eq:K-C} \\
  K_{mn}^{D}
    &= -\oint_{\partial\Omega}\!\oint_{\partial\Omega}
       \bigl(\bm{f}_m\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}\bigr)\,
       \bigl(\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}'\bigr)\,
       G(\bm{r},\bm{r}')\,\dd^{2}r'\,\dd^{2}r.
  \label{eq:K-D}
\end{align}
By the half-SWG property $\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}} = 1$ on $S_n$
and zero on the remaining boundary faces, the surface integrals in
\eqref{eq:K-B}--\eqref{eq:K-D} collapse to integrals over the specific
faces $S_m$ and $S_n$:
$K^{B}_{mn}$ is non-zero only when $n$ is a boundary face,
$K^{C}_{mn}$ only when $m$ is a boundary face, and $K^{D}_{mn}$ only
when both $m$ and $n$ are boundary faces.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Charge neutrality}
\label{sec:charge-neutrality}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The induced bound charge associated with a single half-SWG basis on a
boundary face $S_n$ is the sum of a bulk contribution
$\rho_{\text{vol}} = -\kappa\,\div\bm{D}$ and a surface contribution
$\sigma_{\text{surf}} = \kappa\,\bm{D}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}$. Using
\eqref{eq:swg-div} and the unit-flux property on $S_n$,
\begin{align}
  Q_{\text{vol}}
    &= -\int_{T_n^{+}} \kappa\,\div\bm{f}_n\,\dd^{3}r
     = -\kappa\,\frac{a_n}{V_n^{+}}\cdot V_n^{+}
     = -\kappa\,a_n,
  \label{eq:Q-vol} \\
  Q_{\text{surf}}
    &= \int_{S_n} \kappa\,(\bm{f}_n\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}})\,\dd^{2}r
     = \kappa\,a_n.
  \label{eq:Q-surf}
\end{align}
The sum $Q_{\text{vol}} + Q_{\text{surf}} = 0$ holds exactly
\emph{only} when the surface contributions
$K^{B}, K^{C}, K^{D}$ are retained. Dropping them, as an
internal-only SWG expansion does, leaves the particle with a spurious
net bound charge $-\kappa\,a_n$ per boundary face and produces
cross-section errors of order $10$--$30\%$ on Mie tests. With the full
decomposition \eqref{eq:K-full}, block-VIEM.jl reproduces the Mie sphere
absorption cross section to $\sim 0.2\%$ at $\ell_c = 0.5$ (589~DoFs),
an order-of-magnitude improvement per DoF over cubic-lattice DDA.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Mass matrix}
\label{sec:mass}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The first term in \eqref{eq:Z-decomposition} is the sparse mass matrix
\begin{equation}
  M_{mn}
    = \int_{T_m\cap T_n} \bm{f}_m(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{f}_n(\bm{r})\,\dd^{3}r.
  \label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
The integrand is a degree-$2$ polynomial in each tetrahedron, so the
5-point Gauss rule on the tetrahedron (degree~3 exact) evaluates
$M_{mn}$ without quadrature error.

%======================================================================
\section{Singular and Near-Singular Volume Integration}
\label{sec:duffy}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generalised vertex Duffy transform}
\label{sec:duffy-vertex}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The weakly singular kernel $1/R$ in \eqref{eq:K-A}--\eqref{eq:K-D}
remains integrable but standard Gauss quadrature fails when the source
and test tetrahedra share a vertex, edge, or face, or when the outer
Gauss point approaches a face of the inner element. Following
Mousavi--Sukumar~\cite{MousaviSukumar2010}, the building block for all
such cases is a \emph{vertex-only} Duffy transform. It is applied in
two ways (Sec.~\ref{sec:pair-class}): to the sub-tetrahedra obtained by
subdividing the inner element around an interior singular point, and
at the vertices that the inner element shares with the outer one.

For $\alpha = 1$ (a $1/R$ singularity at a single vertex), the
optimised transform from the unit cube
$\bm{u}=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in[0,1]^{3}$ to barycentric coordinates
$(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3,\lambda_4)$ with $\lambda_1 = 1$ at the
singular vertex is~\cite[Eq.~(1) with $\beta=1$]{MousaviSukumar2010}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_1 = 1 - u_1,\quad
  \lambda_2 = u_1(1-u_2),\quad
  \lambda_3 = u_1 u_2 (1-u_3),\quad
  \lambda_4 = u_1 u_2 u_3.
  \label{eq:duffy-map}
\end{equation}
The face $u_1 = 0$ of the cube collapses entirely to the singular
vertex, and $R \propto u_1$ to leading order along any ray departing
from it. The total Jacobian combining \eqref{eq:duffy-map} with the
affine map from reference to physical coordinates is
\begin{equation}
  J_{D}(\bm{u}) = 6\,\abs{V_{\text{tet}}}\,u_1^{2}\,u_2.
  \label{eq:duffy-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The factor $u_1^{2}$ exactly cancels the $1/R$ singularity, leaving a
smooth integrand on the unit cube that is integrated by a tensor-product
Gauss--Legendre rule. The code uses one rule, $5\times 5\times 5$
(125~points, \texttt{duffy\_reference\_rule(5)}), for every singular and
near-singular volume integral. The outer integral over the test
tetrahedron and all regular inner integrals use the $5$-point Gauss rule
on the tetrahedron.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Pair classification}
\label{sec:pair-class}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Given an outer quadrature point $\bm{r}$ inside the test tetrahedron
$T_m$, the source element $T_n$ is classified by the mesh topology,
that is by the nodes the two tetrahedra share, and not by a distance:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{self} ($T_n = T_m$): the outer Gauss point lies inside
        the source tetrahedron. The source tetrahedron is subdivided
        into four sub-tetrahedra with the outer Gauss point as a common
        vertex, and each is integrated by the Duffy transform
        \eqref{eq:duffy-map}.
  \item \emph{adjacent} ($T_n$ shares a face, an edge or a vertex with
        $T_m$): the outer point lies outside $T_n$ but can be close to
        the shared feature. The kernel is split as
        $G = G_0 + (G - G_0)$ with the static part
        $G_0 = 1/(4\pi R)$. The static part is integrated with the
        Duffy rule placed at each shared vertex of $T_n$ in turn, and
        the results are averaged over the shared vertices. The
        remainder $G - G_0$ is bounded, with the limit
        $i k_0/(4\pi)$ for $R \to 0$, and is integrated with the
        5-point Gauss rule.
  \item \emph{far} (no shared node): the inner integral is evaluated
        with the 5-point Gauss rule. Pairs further apart than the AIM
        near-field radius are never integrated directly
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim}).
\end{itemize}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Surface integrals for $K^{B}, K^{C}, K^{D}$}
\label{sec:surface-int}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The surface contributions in Sec.~\ref{sec:K-BCD} require the
integration of $G$ over a triangle, optionally combined with an inner
tetrahedron integral ($K^{B}, K^{C}$) or an outer triangle integral
($K^{D}$). We adopt a two-dimensional version of the Duffy transform on
the reference triangle: from $(u_1, u_2)\in[0,1]^{2}$ to barycentric
coordinates $(\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3)$,
\begin{equation}
  \eta_1 = 1 - u_1,\quad
  \eta_2 = u_1(1-u_2),\quad
  \eta_3 = u_1 u_2,
  \label{eq:duffy-2d}
\end{equation}
with Jacobian $J_{D,2}(\bm{u}) = 2\abs{A_{\text{tri}}}\,u_1$. The
linear factor $u_1$ cancels the $1/R$ singularity at the vertex $\eta_1
= 1$, and the face $u_1 = 0$ collapses to that vertex just as in the
three-dimensional case. The outer integral always uses a plain Gauss
rule, on the triangle or on the tetrahedron. The inner integral is
chosen by the shared nodes, as in Sec.~\ref{sec:pair-class}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K^{D}$ (triangle--triangle). Same face: the inner triangle is
        subdivided around the outer point and the 2D Duffy rule is
        applied to each sub-triangle. Shared edge or vertex: the 2D
        Duffy rule is placed at each shared vertex of the inner
        triangle and the results are averaged. No shared node: Gauss
        rule.
  \item $K^{B}$ (outer tetrahedron, inner triangle). If the test
        tetrahedron owns the face, the outer Gauss point is projected
        onto the plane of the triangle, the triangle is subdivided
        around the projection and the 2D Duffy rule is applied. If the
        tetrahedron only shares nodes with the face, the 2D Duffy rule
        is placed at the shared vertices and averaged. Otherwise the
        Gauss rule is used.
  \item $K^{C}$ (outer triangle, inner tetrahedron). The inner integral
        is a volume integral of $G$. For the tetrahedron that owns the
        face it uses the subdivision around the outer point with the 3D
        Duffy rule. For a node-sharing tetrahedron it uses the 3D Duffy
        rule at the shared vertices, averaged. Otherwise the 5-point
        Gauss rule is used.
\end{itemize}
The default rules are a collapsed tensor Gauss rule with 4 points per
axis on the triangle and a 2D Duffy rule with 6 points per axis.

%======================================================================
\section{FFT-Accelerated MVP: Adaptive Integral Method}
\label{sec:aim}
%======================================================================

Direct computation and storage of the dense impedance matrix $Z$ scales
as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{2})$ in memory and
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{3})$ in operations for direct factorisation,
quickly becoming prohibitive. The Adaptive Integral Method
(AIM)~\cite{ShengSong} accelerates the matrix--vector product by
representing the far-field part of the kernel on an auxiliary cubic
grid and evaluating the resulting block-Toeplitz sum with a
three-dimensional FFT, mirroring the Goodman--Draine--Flatau
construction~\cite{Goodman1991} used by
\texttt{block-DDA\_Py}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Auxiliary grid and stencil}
\label{sec:aim-grid}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A Cartesian grid of spacing $h_g$ is overlaid on the bounding box of
the particle. Let $\bm{g}_p$ denote the position of grid point $p$.
Each basis function $\bm{f}_n$ is associated with a stencil
$\mathcal{S}_n$ of $M_{\text{st}} = (n_{\text{st}}+1)^3$ nearby grid
points surrounding its centroid. For a moment matching order
$n_{\text{st}}$, the stencil is a cube with
$n_{\text{st}}+1$ grid points on each side.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Moment matching}
\label{sec:aim-moments}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For each basis function $\bm{f}_n$ and each stencil point
$p\in\mathcal{S}_n$ we construct a projection weight
$W_{np}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}$ so that the multipole moments of the point
source at $p$ match those of the continuous source $\bm{f}_n$ up to
order $n_{\text{st}}$. The moments are defined with respect to the
basis centroid $\bm{c}_n$:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_n^{\bm{\alpha}}
    = \int_{\Omega}\bm{f}_n(\bm{r})\,(\bm{r}-\bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\,
      \dd^{3}r,
  \qquad
  \abs{\bm{\alpha}}=\alpha_x+\alpha_y+\alpha_z \le n_{\text{st}},
  \label{eq:aim-moments}
\end{equation}
and the weights $\{W_{np}\}_{p\in\mathcal{S}_n}$ are defined by the
linear constraint
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}_n} W_{np}\,(\bm{g}_p - \bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}
    = \mu_n^{\bm{\alpha}},
  \qquad \forall\,\abs{\bm{\alpha}}\le n_{\text{st}}.
  \label{eq:aim-matching}
\end{equation}
A companion set of weights matches the divergence moments
$\int (\div\bm{f}_n)(\bm{r}-\bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\,\dd^{3}r$ for the
scalar potential part of the kernel. The linear system
\eqref{eq:aim-matching} is small ($M_{\text{st}}$ unknowns per basis
function, $\tbinom{n_{\text{st}}+3}{3}$ equations) and is solved once,
during matrix setup, per basis function.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Far-field product via FFT}
\label{sec:aim-fft}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

With the moment matching complete, the far-field part of the
matrix--vector product $\bm{y}^{\text{far}} = K^{\text{far}}\,\bm{x}$ is
evaluated in three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Projection.} For each basis function $n$ and each stencil
        point $p$, accumulate
        $q_p = \sum_n W_{np}\,x_n$ onto the grid.
  \item \emph{Convolution.} Compute
        $Q_p = \sum_{p'} G(\bm{g}_p - \bm{g}_{p'})\,q_{p'}$ via a
        three-dimensional FFT of $q$, pointwise multiplication by the
        precomputed FFT of $G$ on the doubled grid, and an inverse FFT.
  \item \emph{Interpolation.} For each test function $m$,
        $y_m^{\text{far}} = \sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}_m} W_{mp}^{\top}\,Q_p$.
\end{enumerate}
Because $G$ depends only on the grid-point separation, its convolution
matrix is block-Toeplitz and is computed with the $3$D FFT of the same
construction used in \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}~\cite{Goodman1991}. The cost
is $\mathcal{O}(N_g \log N_g)$ per MVP, where $N_g$ is the number of
grid points. Memory for the grid Green function is also
$\mathcal{O}(N_g)$ on the doubled domain.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Precorrection of near-field pairs}
\label{sec:aim-precorrection}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The FFT sum above is accurate only in the far field; for pairs with
$\norm{\bm{c}_m - \bm{c}_n} \le r_{\text{near}}$ it is replaced by the
exact integrals of Sec.~\ref{sec:duffy}. The construction is a
\emph{difference} of two quantities: for each near pair $(m,n)$ we
compute the exact $K_{mn}$ and subtract from it the contribution that
the FFT would have produced through the grid projection, storing the
difference in a sparse precorrection matrix $K^{\text{pc}}$. The
complete MVP is
\begin{equation}
  \bm{y} = \bigl(D_M - \tfrac{\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
              (K^{\text{far}} + K^{\text{pc}})\bigr)\,\bm{x},
  \label{eq:aim-mvp}
\end{equation}
where $D_M = M/\epsilon_p$ is the sparse mass contribution (for an
anisotropic particle $\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha^{-1} M_\alpha$,
Sec.~\ref{sec:anisotropic}) and $K^{\text{pc}}$ has
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}})$ non-zero entries. Only the upper triangle
of the symmetric $K^{\text{pc}}$ is stored. The default near radius
is $r_{\text{near}} = 2 \times (\text{stencil extent})$, verified in
Phase-3 benchmarks to produce $<0.5\%$ relative error between the
AIM-accelerated and the dense matrix--vector products.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Surface-moment extension for $K^{B}$, $K^{C}$, $K^{D}$}
\label{sec:aim-surface}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The projection of Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-moments} accelerates only the
bulk--bulk kernel $K^{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:K-A}. The three boundary-face
contributions $K^{B}, K^{C}, K^{D}$ of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:K-B}--\eqref{eq:K-D}
are, in the Phase-1a release, stored as a separate sparse matrix
\texttt{half\_swg\_extra} of dimension $N_{\text{bnd}} \times
N_{\text{dof}}$, which for the iron-oxide doublet geometry dominates
total live memory at $81$--$86\%$ (measured at R/5, R/6 mesh
refinement). This subsection extends the moment-matching projection to
the boundary-face supports so that $K^{B}, K^{C}, K^{D}$ share the same
three-dimensional FFT convolution as $K^{A}$. Their near-field part is
then a sparse correction with $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{bnd}})$ entries
rather than $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{bnd}} N_{\text{dof}})$, and it is
stored in the precorrection matrix $K^{\text{pc}}$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-precorrection}. The separate matrix is no longer
filled: the field \texttt{half\_swg\_extra} of the operator remains as
an empty placeholder. The construction follows
Anastassiu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{Anastassiu1998}, which supplies the
mathematical rigour (``thin-sheet constraint'') omitted from the
original AIM presentation of Bleszynski \emph{et~al.}~\cite{Bleszynski1996}.

\subsubsection*{Scalar surface density on a volumetric grid}

Inspection of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:K-B}--\eqref{eq:K-D} shows that the three
boundary kernels couple to a \emph{single} scalar surface density
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_n(\bm{r}) \equiv \bm{f}_n(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}}(\bm{r}),
  \qquad \bm{r}\in S_n,
  \label{eq:surf-density}
\end{equation}
supported on the single boundary triangle $S_n$ of basis $n$; by the
half-SWG unit-flux property, $\sigma_n = 1$ on $S_n$ and vanishes
elsewhere on $\partial\Omega$. Because the divergence theorem of
Sec.~\ref{sec:K-BCD} has already contracted the vector basis
$\bm{f}_n$ against the outward normal, the surface AIM projection
requires neither Cartesian components nor a surface divergence of
$\bm{f}_n$: only the scalar $\sigma_n$ appears. This is a substantial
simplification over the four-channel RWG projection of
Anastassiu~\cite{Anastassiu1998}, where no preceding integration by
parts is performed.

The triangle $S_n$ is a two-dimensional manifold embedded in the
three-dimensional grid $\{\bm{g}_p\}$. A naive surface moment
$\int_{S_n}\sigma_n\,(\bm{r}-\bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\,\dd^{2}r$
determines the grid projection only for multi-indices $\bm{\alpha}$
with zero normal component; moments with $\alpha_{\perp} \ge 1$ (where
$\perp$ denotes the local direction along $\hat{\bm{n}}$) remain
unconstrained, and the projection is consequently rank-deficient on a
volumetric grid. Following Anastassiu~\cite{Anastassiu1998}, we
\emph{thicken} $\sigma_n$ to a volumetric distribution by a normal
delta,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\sigma}_n(\bm{r})
    \equiv \sigma_n(\bm{r}_{\|})\,\delta(\xi_n),
  \qquad
  \xi_n = (\bm{r} - \bm{r}_{S_n})\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{n}},
  \label{eq:thickening}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{r}_{\|}$ is the tangential projection of $\bm{r}$ onto the
plane of $S_n$ and $\bm{r}_{S_n}$ is any reference point on $S_n$. The
three-dimensional moments of $\tilde{\sigma}_n$ are then
\begin{equation}
  \mu_n^{\bm{\alpha},S}
    \;\equiv\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
      \tilde{\sigma}_n(\bm{r})\,
      (\bm{r} - \bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\,\dd^{3}r
    \;=\; \int_{S_n}
      \sigma_n(\bm{r}_s)\,
      (\bm{r}_s - \bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\,\dd^{2}r_s,
  \label{eq:surf-moments}
\end{equation}
where the right-hand side is evaluated in the global Cartesian basis
so that components along $\hat{\bm{n}}$ with $\alpha_{\perp} \ge 1$
automatically vanish and enter the linear system
\eqref{eq:surf-matching} below as the ``thin-sheet constraint''
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}^{S}_n}\Lambda^{S}_{nq}\,
    \bigl(\bm{g}_q - \bm{c}_n\bigr)^{\bm{\alpha}} = 0
  \quad\text{for all}\ \bm{\alpha}\ \text{with}\ \alpha_{\perp}\ge 1,
  \label{eq:thin-sheet}
\end{equation}
forcing the projected point sources to collapse to a zero-thickness
sheet in the grid's far-field representation.

\subsubsection*{Moment matching}

In direct analogy with the bulk matching equation
\eqref{eq:aim-matching}, the surface projection weights
$\{\Lambda^{S}_{nq}\}_{q \in \mathcal{S}^{S}_n}$ are determined by
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{q \in \mathcal{S}^{S}_n}
    \Lambda^{S}_{nq}\,(\bm{g}_q - \bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}
  = \mu_n^{\bm{\alpha},S},
  \qquad \forall\,\abs{\bm{\alpha}} \le n_{\text{st}},
  \label{eq:surf-matching}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}^{S}_n$ is a cubic stencil of
$M_{\text{st}} = (n_{\text{st}}+1)^{3}$ grid nodes. The expansion
centre $\bm{c}_n$ and the stencil are those of the bulk channels of the
same basis function: $\bm{c}_n$ is the volume-weighted centroid of the
supporting tetrahedra, which for a half-SWG function is the centroid of
its tetrahedron. The surface and bulk contributions therefore share one
grid and one FFT plan. The Vandermonde-type matrix
$\bigl[(\bm{g}_q-\bm{c}_n)^{\bm{\alpha}}\bigr]$ is of size
$\binom{n_{\text{st}}+3}{3}\times M_{\text{st}}$ and is independent of
the kernel $G$. It depends on the position of the stencil relative to
$\bm{c}_n$, so it is built once per basis function and solved for the
bulk and for the surface right-hand sides.

\subsubsection*{Far-field product via the shared FFT}

With the bulk vector weights $W^{\text{vec},\alpha}_{np}$ (three
Cartesian components), the bulk divergence weights $W^{\div}_{np}$,
and the new surface weights $\Lambda^{S}_{nq}$, the full far-field MVP
$\bm{y}^{\text{far}} = K^{\text{far}}\bm{x}$ proceeds through a single
grid pass. The divergence and the surface density enter only through
their difference, so they are combined into one effective scalar
source:
\begin{equation}
  q^{\text{vec},\alpha}_p = \sum_{n} W^{\text{vec},\alpha}_{np}\,x_n,
  \qquad
  q^{\text{sc}}_p = \sum_{n} W^{\div}_{np}\,x_n
    - \sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{bnd}}} \Lambda^{S}_{np}\,x_n,
  \label{eq:aim-surf-proj}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_{\text{bnd}}$ is the index set of half-SWG basis
functions with a boundary face. One three-dimensional FFT convolution
with the Green-function kernel is applied to each of the four scalar
source fields,
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\bullet}_p = \sum_{p'} G(\bm{g}_p - \bm{g}_{p'})\,q^{\bullet}_{p'},
  \qquad
  \bullet\in\{\text{vec},x;\ \text{vec},y;\ \text{vec},z;\ \text{sc}\},
  \label{eq:aim-surf-fft}
\end{equation}
reusing the precomputed FFT of $G$ exactly as in
Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-fft}. The test-side interpolation uses the same
combination of weights,
\begin{equation}
  y_m^{\text{far}}
  = k_0^{2}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}_m}
     W^{\text{vec},\alpha}_{mp}\,Q^{\text{vec},\alpha}_p
  \;-\; \sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}_m}
     \bigl(W^{\div}_{mp} - \bigl[m\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{bnd}}\bigr]\,
           \Lambda^{S}_{mp}\bigr)\,Q^{\text{sc}}_p ,
  \label{eq:aim-surf-mvp}
\end{equation}
with the Iverson bracket $[\,\cdot\,]$. Expanding the product of the two
differences gives the four contributions: $W^{\div}$ with $W^{\div}$ is
the divergence--divergence piece of $K^{A}$, the two mixed terms are
$K^{B}$ and $K^{C}$, and $\Lambda^{S}$ with $\Lambda^{S}$ is $K^{D}$. The
appearance of the same difference on the source and on the test side
reflects the charge-neutrality identity
$Q_{\text{vol}} + Q_{\text{surf}} = 0$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:charge-neutrality}: the bulk divergence and the
boundary-face normal flux are the two halves of a single conserved
charge distribution, and the AIM projection preserves this
cancellation on the grid.

No additional FFT is required compared to the bulk-only pipeline of
Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-fft}: the surface density is added to the divergence
channel, for a total of $3 + 1 = 4$ scalar FFT convolutions per MVP
(three vector components and one effective scalar source).

\subsubsection*{Near-field correction}

For pairs $(m,n)$ with at least one of $\{m,n\}$ on a boundary face
and $\norm{\bm{c}_m - \bm{c}_n}\le r_{\text{near}}$, the far-field
approximation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:aim-surf-mvp} is insufficient. In
direct analogy with the bulk precorrection of
Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-precorrection}, the difference between the directly
integrated $K^{A}+K^{B}+K^{C}+K^{D}$ of a near pair and its
AIM-reconstructed value is stored in the same sparse matrix
$K^{\text{pc}}$. No separate block is kept for the surface terms. The
number of additional entries scales as
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{bnd}}\,\langle M^{S}_{\text{near}}\rangle)$,
where $\langle M^{S}_{\text{near}}\rangle$ is the mean number of
near-neighbours per boundary face. For a mesh with characteristic
edge length $\ell_c$ and near radius
$r_{\text{near}} = 2 \times (\text{stencil extent})$, this count is
$\mathcal{O}(1)$. On compact objects with
$N_{\text{bnd}} = \mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{2/3})$, this is a
reduction from $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{5/3})$
(\texttt{half\_swg\_extra} in Phase-1a) to
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{2/3})$---an asymptotic factor of
$N_{\text{dof}}$.

\subsubsection*{Evaluation of the surface moments}

With $\sigma_n = 1$ on $S_n$ the surface moment
\eqref{eq:surf-moments} is the integral of a monomial of degree
$\abs{\bm{\alpha}} \le n_{\text{st}}$ over a planar triangle. It is
evaluated with the triangle Gauss rule used for the surface integrals
(a collapsed tensor rule with 4 points per axis by default). This rule
is exact for polynomials of this degree, so the surface moments carry
no quadrature error beyond rounding. The bulk moments are polynomial
integrals over tetrahedra and are evaluated with the 5-point rule.

\subsubsection*{Summary of Phase A impedance}

With the surface projection in place, the AIM-accelerated impedance
equation keeps the form \eqref{eq:aim-mvp},
\begin{equation}
  \bm{y} = \bigl(D_M
      - \tfrac{\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
        \bigl(K^{\text{far}} + K^{\text{pc}}\bigr)
    \bigr)\,\bm{x},
  \label{eq:aim-mvp-phaseA}
\end{equation}
where $K^{\text{far}}$ now evaluates all four kernel contributions
$K^{A}+K^{B}+K^{C}+K^{D}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:aim-surf-mvp} and
$K^{\text{pc}}$ holds the near-field correction of all four. The
Phase-1a \texttt{half\_swg\_extra} matrix is no longer filled.

\subsubsection*{Memory and timing scaling}

The Au doublet benchmark
(\mbox{$R_{\text{monomer}} = 30$~nm}, gap \SI{3}{nm}, $\lambda_0 =
\SI{638}{nm}$, Johnson--Christy~\cite{JohnsonChristy1972} gold)
measured at four refinement levels $\ell_c = R/k$ for
$k \in \{5, 6, 7, 8\}$ shows the total in-memory \texttt{AIMOperator}
footprint growing as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{1.02})$ ---
essentially linear in $N_{\text{dof}}$, confirming the Phase A
design target.  The dominant contribution is the bulk precorrection
block $K^{\text{pc}}$ (\SIrange{69}{72}{\percent} of total), whose
sparsity scales as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}\,n_{\text{near}})$
with $n_{\text{near}} \lesssim (M_{\text{st}}\,r_{\text{near}}/
\ell_c)^3$ an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ count independent of
$N_{\text{dof}}$.  The new surface projection $W^{S}$ adds
\SIrange{4}{5}{\percent} to the total, a small overhead compared
with the eliminated $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{5/3})$ Phase-1a
\texttt{half\_swg\_extra} matrix.  Setup and solve times also scale
as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}})$ within measurement noise,
consistent with the AIM MVP cost $\mathcal{O}(N_g \log N_g +
N_{\text{dof}}\,n_{\text{near}})$ at fixed wavelength.  At $R/7$
the operator footprint is below \SI{300}{MiB} and at $R/8$ below
\SI{450}{MiB}, so further refinement on a \SI{16}{GB} workstation
is feasible and is the next step toward closing the residual
$\Im S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ error at $\beta = \pi/2$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:doublet-mstm}).  The full per-row table — DoF count,
$N_{\text{bnd}}$, per-component memory, $t_{\text{setup}}$,
$t_{\text{solve}}$ — is in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 8.3.

\section{Solvers}
\label{sec:solver}
%======================================================================

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Direct and Krylov solvers}
\label{sec:direct-krylov}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For small problems ($N_{\text{dof}} \lesssim$ a few~thousand), the
dense assembly of $Z$ and its LU factorisation is practical and serves
as a validation reference for the AIM path. For larger problems we use
a Krylov iterative method applied to the AIM operator
\eqref{eq:aim-mvp}. The production path is the block GMRES of
Sec.~\ref{sec:block-krylov}, which solves all orientations at once.
The single right-hand-side function \texttt{solve\_iterative} uses
BiCGSTAB from \texttt{Krylov.jl}~\cite{KrylovJl}. No preconditioner is
applied by default. Right preconditioning with the mass matrix is
available as an option (Sec.~\ref{sec:shift-precond}).

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Multi-orientation batch solve}
\label{sec:multi-orient}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Because the impedance matrix $Z$ depends only on the particle geometry
and permittivity, it can be assembled once and then reused across many
orientations: each orientation changes only the right-hand side
$\bm{b}$. The function \texttt{solve\_cas\_v2\_orientations} is the
user-facing entry point; by default it selects
\texttt{method=:aim\_gmres} (since v0.7.1), which builds the AIM operator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:aim}) once and then solves the block system
$\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{B}$ for all orientations simultaneously
via a single block-Krylov iteration
(Sec.~\ref{sec:block-krylov}). For small problems
($N_{\text{dof}}\lesssim 10^3$) \texttt{method=:dense} falls back to
the textbook assemble-$Z$-and-LU-factorise path, back-substituting for
every $(\alpha_\ell,\beta_\ell,\gamma_\ell)$ in the input list. For
spheroids either route is especially efficient: only the $\alpha = 0$
orientation needs an independent solve for each $\beta$, and the
remaining $\alpha$ values are obtained by the analytical expansion of
Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha-expansion}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parallel execution}
\label{sec:parallel}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Every computationally significant loop in the setup, MVP, and
post-processing paths is parallelised for shared-memory multi-core
execution. The user controls the number of worker threads with
\texttt{julia -t N} (or the \texttt{JULIA\_NUM\_THREADS} environment
variable); no other configuration is required.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The precorrection assembly (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-precorrection}) is the
dominant setup cost for any realistic problem ---
$\gtrsim$\SI{90}{\percent} of \texttt{build\_aim\_operator} wall time
at $N_{\text{dof}}\gtrsim 10^3$. Its outer near-column loop is
partitioned into chunks and launched as independent
\texttt{Threads.@spawn} tasks; each task accumulates its own COO
triplets and the resulting \texttt{SparseMatrixCSC} is built once
from the concatenated lists. The same pattern parallelises
\texttt{build\_aim\_projection} (basis loop). The dense assembly uses a
row-parallel \texttt{Threads.@threads} loop. BLAS is forced to a
single thread inside the parallel region to avoid nested
oversubscription from LAPACK calls (the monomial-matrix
least-squares solves inside \texttt{build\_aim\_projection}). On an
Intel Xeon 20-core machine against a $N_{\text{swg}}\!=\!3041$
sphere mesh, \texttt{assemble\_precorrection} drops from
\SI{25.9}{\second} (1 thread) to \SI{10.5}{\second} (4 threads,
$2.5\times$) and \SI{8.9}{\second} (10 threads, $2.9\times$).

\paragraph{Block MVP.}
The column loop in \texttt{aim\_mvp(op, X)} is parallelised: each of
the $L$ right-hand-side columns is dispatched as its own task, so
for a machine with $N_{\text{thr}}$ Julia threads the FFT work of
the block MVP runs at effective width
$\min(L,N_{\text{thr}})$. Details in
Sec.~\ref{sec:block-krylov}.

\paragraph{FFT.}
FFTW maintains its own thread pool; at module load
\texttt{BlockVIEM} sets it to \texttt{Threads.nthreads()} via
\texttt{FFTW.set\_num\_threads}. The exported
\texttt{BlockVIEM.set\_fft\_threads(n)} resets it at run time. The
recommended balance for a block MVP with $L$ columns is
\begin{equation}
  n_{\text{FFT}} \;=\;
    \max\!\bigl(1,\;\lceil N_{\text{thr}} / L\rceil\bigr),
  \label{eq:fft-threads-rule}
\end{equation}
so that the column and FFT parallelism together saturate the cores
without oversubscription.

\paragraph{Parameter-sweep reuse.}
The projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_{x,y,z},
\mathbf{W}_{\text{div}},\mathbf{W}_{\text{surf}}$ and the mass
matrix $\mathbf{M}$ depend only on the mesh, the grid pitch and
padding, and the polynomial order --- not on $k_0$ or $\epsilon_p$.
The \texttt{build\_aim\_operator} constructor accepts keyword
arguments \texttt{projection} and \texttt{mass} so that wavelength
or material sweeps can build those pieces once and reuse them,
rebuilding only the Green-function FFT kernel and the precorrection
for each new $(k_0,\epsilon_p)$. The MVP is bit-identical between
the fresh and reused paths (tolerance $< 10^{-13}$).

\section{Scattering Observables}
\label{sec:observables}
%======================================================================

Let $D_n$ ($n = 1,\ldots,N_{\text{dof}}$) denote the solution of
\eqref{eq:linear-system} and $\bm{D}(\bm{r}) = \sum D_n\,\bm{f}_n(\bm{r})$
the reconstructed electric flux density. This section collects the
observables computed from $D_n$ that correspond to the CAS-v2 detector
protocol~\cite{Moteki2024} and can be compared directly with
\texttt{block-DDA\_Py}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Far-field scattering amplitude}
\label{sec:far-field}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The scattered far field in the physics convention reads
\begin{equation}
  \bm{E}^{\text{sca}}(\bm{r}) \;\sim\;
    \frac{\mathrm{e}^{+i k_0 r}}{4\pi\,r}\,\bm{F}(\hat{\bm{r}}),
  \qquad r\to\infty,
  \label{eq:far-field}
\end{equation}
where the vector scattering amplitude is obtained by transverse
projection of the Fourier transform of $\bm{D}$:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F}(\hat{\bm{r}})
    = \frac{k_0^{2}\,\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
      \bigl(\mathbf{I}_3 - \hat{\bm{r}}\otimes\hat{\bm{r}}\bigr)\cdot
      \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{dof}}} D_n
      \int_{\Omega}\bm{f}_n(\bm{r}')\,
                    \mathrm{e}^{-i k_0\,\hat{\bm{r}}\cdot\bm{r}'}\,
      \dd^{3}r'.
  \label{eq:F-viem}
\end{equation}
The standard scattering amplitude used in Bohren--Huffman
\cite{Bohren1983} is $\bm{f}(\hat{\bm{r}}) = \bm{F}(\hat{\bm{r}})/(4\pi)$.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Absorption cross section}
\label{sec:C-abs}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The absorption cross section is evaluated directly from the volumetric
Joule-loss formula, which is bilinear in $\bm{D}$ and hence reduces to
a quadratic form in the expansion coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{abs}}
    = \frac{k_0\,\Im(\epsilon_p)}
           {\epsilon_{\text{bg}}\,\abs{\epsilon_p}^{2}\,\abs{\bm{E}_0}^{2}}\,
      \Re\!\bigl(\bm{D}^{\dagger}\mathbf{M}\,\bm{D}\bigr),
  \label{eq:C-abs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the mass matrix \eqref{eq:mass}. For real
$\epsilon_p$ this identically vanishes.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scattering cross section by far-field integration}
\label{sec:C-sca}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The scattering cross section is computed as the integral of the
radiated flux density over the unit sphere,
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{sca}}
    = \frac{1}{16\pi^{2}\,\abs{\bm{E}_0}^{2}}
      \int_{S^{2}} \abs{\bm{F}(\hat{\bm{r}})}^{2}\,\dd\Omega,
  \label{eq:C-sca}
\end{equation}
using a product Gauss--Legendre (in $\cos\theta$) times trapezoid (in
$\phi$) rule with $2 n_{\theta}^{2}$ directions. The default
$n_{\theta} = 10$ is sufficient for size parameters
$k_0\,r_{ve}\lesssim 5$; larger targets use $n_\theta\ge 20$. The
quadratic functional $\abs{\bm{F}}^{2}$ is nonnegative and insensitive
to the sign-convention subtleties that plague forward-direction
optical-theorem estimates of $C_{\text{ext}}$, so in practice we obtain
$C_{\text{ext}}$ from
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{ext}} = C_{\text{abs}} + C_{\text{sca}}.
  \label{eq:C-ext}
\end{equation}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{PCAS forward amplitude}
\label{sec:S-fw}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The CAS-v2 forward scattering observables are defined with a circular
incident polarisation~\cite{Moteki2024}. Let
$\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{fw}}$ be the forward scattering direction (equal
to the incident propagation direction) and
$\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{fw}},\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{fw}}$ the
associated polar and azimuthal unit vectors in the particle frame. The
$s$- and $p$-channel PCAS forward observables are
\begin{align}
  S_{\text{fw},\theta}^{\text{PCAS}}\;[=S_s(0)]
    &= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4\pi}\,
       \bm{F}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{fw}})\cdot\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{fw}},
  \label{eq:S-fw-theta} \\
  S_{\text{fw},\phi}^{\text{PCAS}}\;[=S_p(0)]
    &= -\,\frac{i\,\sqrt{2}}{4\pi}\,
       \bm{F}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{fw}})\cdot\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{fw}},
  \label{eq:S-fw-phi}
\end{align}
where the $\sqrt{2}$ and $i\sqrt{2}$ factors are inherited from the
circular polarisation decomposition \eqref{eq:circular-pol}, and the
$1/(4\pi)$ factor converts from $\bm{F}$ to the BH83-normalised
scattering amplitude $\bm{f}=\bm{F}/(4\pi)$. Equations
\eqref{eq:S-fw-theta} and \eqref{eq:S-fw-phi} are the two physical
quantities measured by the PCAS instrument. For convenience, their
arithmetic mean
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{fw,mean}}^{\text{PCAS}}
    \;=\; \frac{S_{\text{fw},\theta}^{\text{PCAS}}
                + S_{\text{fw},\phi}^{\text{PCAS}}}{2}
  \label{eq:S-fw-combined}
\end{equation}
is also stored in the \texttt{CASv2Result} container as a derived
single-channel summary; \eqref{eq:S-fw-theta} and \eqref{eq:S-fw-phi}
remain the primary observables.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{OCBS backward amplitude}
\label{sec:S-bk}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For the exact backward scattering direction
$\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{bk}} = -\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{inc}}$, the laboratory
basis is
$\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{bk}} = -\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta,\text{inc}}$
and $\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{bk}} = +\hat{\bm{e}}_{\phi,\text{inc}}$.
The $\theta$- and $\phi$-channel backward amplitudes are defined in
direct analogy with \eqref{eq:S-fw-theta}--\eqref{eq:S-fw-phi} at
$\hat{\bm{r}} = \hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{bk}}$, and the single observable
reported by the OCBS (Optical Coherence Backscattering Sensor)
instrument~\cite{Moteki2024} is the linear combination
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{bk}}^{\text{OCBS}}
    = \frac{-\,S_{\text{bk},\theta}^{\text{OCBS}}
            + S_{\text{bk},\phi}^{\text{OCBS}}}{2}.
  \label{eq:S-bk}
\end{equation}
In terms of the amplitude-matrix elements at the backward direction the
two channels are
$S_{\text{bk},\theta}^{\text{OCBS}} = S_{11}(\pi) + i\,S_{12}(\pi)$ and
$S_{\text{bk},\phi}^{\text{OCBS}} = S_{22}(\pi) - i\,S_{21}(\pi)$.
The backscattering theorem $S_{21}(\pi) = -S_{12}(\pi)$ cancels the
off-diagonal elements in \eqref{eq:S-bk}, so that
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{bk}}^{\text{OCBS}}
    = \frac{1}{2}\,\bigl[-S_{11}(\pi) + S_{22}(\pi)\bigr].
  \label{eq:S-bk-amplitude-matrix}
\end{equation}
This is the backward amplitude $S_{\text{bak}}$ of the CAS-v2 protocol in
the corrected form of Eq.~(28) of Ref.~\cite{Moteki2024}, which is given
in the erratum to that paper~\cite{Moteki2024Erratum}.

The factor $1/2$ has the following origin.
The quarter-wave plate in the return path has its axes at \ang{45} to
the linear polarisations.
Each of the two backscattered components therefore reaches the output
polarisation with the amplitude factor $1/\sqrt{2}$.
Together with the factor $1/\sqrt{2}$ of the circularly polarised
incident wave this gives $1/2$.
The printed Eq.~(28) of Ref.~\cite{Moteki2024} adds the two components
at full amplitude and has $1/\sqrt{2}$ instead.
For a sphere $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$ and $S_{22}(\pi) = -S_{11}(\pi)$,
so \eqref{eq:S-bk-amplitude-matrix} reduces to
$S_{\text{bk}}^{\text{OCBS}} = S_{22}(\pi)$.
In the Rayleigh limit $S_{\text{bk}}^{\text{OCBS}}$ equals the forward
amplitude.

Versions of block-VIEM.jl up to 0.7.9 used the printed equation.
Their $S_{\text{bk}}^{\text{OCBS}}$, including the Mie, MSTM and
T-matrix reference values, is $\sqrt{2}$ times the present one.
The phase is the same, and so is the ratio of a VIEM value to its
reference value.
The stored files under \texttt{viem\_results/paper/} were produced with
the printed equation and are kept as produced.
block-DDA\_Py follows the corrected equation from v0.8.2.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
%======================================================================

The block-VIEM.jl solver has been validated against four independent
references: the Mie series for homogeneous spheres
(dielectric and plasmonic gold), an analytical $\alpha$-symmetry
identity for axisymmetric particles
(Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha-expansion}), the multi-sphere T-matrix
package \textsc{MSTMforCAS.jl}~\cite{MSTMforCAS} on a touching
two-sphere doublet, and \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}~\cite{Moteki2024} on
the spheroid, GRE ($\beta > 0$), and anisotropic-$\varepsilon_p$
test cases.  Sub-1\,\% accuracy on all five CAS-v2 observables is
reached at modest mesh sizes ($N \sim 2 \times 10^3$ half-SWG DoF)
for the Mie reference, and agreement with the independent codes is
at the few-percent level expected from the discretisation budget of
either side.  Mesh refinement on the plasmonic doublet recovers
the theoretical linear-SWG convergence rate $p \approx 2$ on every
observable.

The full set of validation conditions (geometry, material, mesh,
solver settings), per-observable error tables (real and imaginary
parts of $S_{\text{fw},\theta}$, $S_{\text{fw},\phi}$,
$S_{\text{bk}}$, plus $C_{\text{ext/abs/sca}}$), the
$\ell_c$-refinement convergence-rate fits, and the AIM-operator
memory scaling are collected in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md}; that document is the
authoritative numerical record for citation in the paper.  This
section retains only the theoretical identity used to define the
$\alpha$-symmetry check (Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha-expansion}) and the
asymptotic cost model used to reason about VIEM-vs-DDA per-DoF
efficiency (Sec.~\ref{sec:viem-vs-dda}); their associated numerical
tables live in the benchmark document.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Oblate spheroid $\alpha$-symmetry}
\label{sec:alpha-expansion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A prolate or oblate spheroid has a continuous $C_\infty$ rotation
symmetry about its figure axis. When the orientation is parameterised
by intrinsic ZYZ Euler angles with $\gamma = 0$, rotating the first
angle $\alpha$ does not change the physical orientation of an
axisymmetric particle in the particle frame; it only rotates the
incident polarisation basis by $\alpha$ about
$\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{inc}}$. For the CAS-v2 observables this implies
the analytical $\alpha$-expansion~\cite{Moteki2024}
\begin{align}
  S_\theta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma{=}0)
    &= A(\beta) + B(\beta)\,\mathrm{e}^{+2 i \alpha},
  \label{eq:alpha-exp-theta}\\
  S_\phi(\alpha,\beta,\gamma{=}0)
    &= A(\beta) - B(\beta)\,\mathrm{e}^{+2 i \alpha},
  \label{eq:alpha-exp-phi}
\end{align}
with
\begin{equation}
  A(\beta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[S_\theta(0,\beta,0)+S_\phi(0,\beta,0)\bigr],
  \quad
  B(\beta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[S_\theta(0,\beta,0)-S_\phi(0,\beta,0)\bigr].
  \label{eq:AB}
\end{equation}
The identity \eqref{eq:alpha-exp-theta}--\eqref{eq:alpha-exp-phi} is the
exact basis of the $\alpha$-analytical expansion that
\texttt{block-DDA\_Py} uses to reduce the orientation sweep to solves at
$\alpha = 0$ only. Because VIEM after the physics-convention switch
lives in the same time-convention as block-DDA\_Py, the sign of the
exponent is $+2i\alpha$ --- identical to block-DDA\_Py.

The benchmark script
\texttt{benchmarks/cas\_v2/spheroid\_ar3.jl} verifies
\eqref{eq:alpha-exp-theta}--\eqref{eq:alpha-exp-phi} numerically by
solving an oblate spheroid (\textsc{bc\_ratio} $= 3$) at
$\beta = \pi/4$ for $\alpha\in\{0,\pi/8,\pi/4,3\pi/8,\pi/2\}$ and
comparing each independently solved amplitude with the analytical
prediction; the residual is at the level of double-precision
machine error (see \texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 3).  The
$\alpha$-symmetry check is internally consistent only and does not
confirm absolute amplitudes; that is done by direct
cross-validation against \texttt{block-DDA\_Py} on the same
spheroid, on GRE shapes with $\beta > 0$, and on anisotropic
$\varepsilon_p$ spheres
(\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S\S 4--6).

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Accuracy and computational cost vs.\ block-DDA\_Py}
\label{sec:viem-vs-dda}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

On the homogeneous Mie sphere ($m_p = 1.5 + 0.01 i$,
$\lambda_0 = \SI{10}{\micro\metre}$, $r = \SI{1}{\micro\metre}$,
$x \approx 0.63$) block-VIEM.jl is roughly \textbf{10--12$\times$
more accurate per DoF} than block-DDA\_Py: matching the
$\sim 1.25\%$ DDA $C_{\text{abs}}$ error obtained at $\sim 4500$
dipoles requires only $\sim 400$ half-SWG DoFs, and at $N \sim 8000$
the VIEM error is an order of magnitude smaller.  The full
per-DoF accuracy table and the high-contrast $m_p = 3.17 + 0.16 i$
extension are tabulated in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 9.

There are four mutually reinforcing reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Conforming geometry.} A tetrahedral mesh represents
        curved boundaries to $O(\ell_c^{2})$, whereas a cubic lattice
        stair-cases them to $O(d)$. For Mie spheres this alone removes
        a geometric contribution to $C_{\text{abs}}$ and
        $C_{\text{sca}}$ that scales as $d$ rather than $d^{2}$.
  \item \emph{Conforming vector space.} The SWG/half-SWG basis is
        $H(\mathrm{div})$-conforming, so the normal component of
        $\bm{D}$ is automatically continuous across tetrahedron faces
        --- the physically correct boundary condition at a dielectric
        interface. Expanding $\bm{D}$ instead of
        $\bm{E}$~\cite[Sec.~II.B]{SWG1984} further ensures that the
        polarisation jump at $\partial\Omega$ is represented exactly
        by the half-SWG functions on the boundary faces, with the
        charge-neutrality identity of Sec.~\ref{sec:charge-neutrality}.
        In DDA the induced polarisation is piecewise constant at the
        dipole centres, so the interface condition is only recovered
        in the limit $d\to 0$.
  \item \emph{Linear vector basis vs point dipoles.} Within each
        tetrahedron $\bm{D}$ is represented by up to four SWG
        functions, each linear in position and with a piecewise-constant
        divergence. DDA represents the polarisation by a single point
        dipole per element, i.e.\ a delta distribution localised at
        the lattice node. The tetrahedral moment approximation is
        therefore intrinsically one order higher, and in the far field
        the error per element scales as $k_0 h$ rather than
        $(k_0 d)^{0}$.
  \item \emph{Physical polarisability.} DDA requires a dynamic
        polarisability prescription (Clausius--Mossotti plus the
        $(2/3)(ka)^{3}$ radiative correction~\cite{Draine1988}) to
        cancel leading-order interaction errors, and the choice of
        prescription feeds back into the error. VIEM solves the
        integral equation directly; $\bm{D}$ is the physical variable,
        and no such tuning is required.
\end{enumerate}

Against these accuracy advantages, block-VIEM.jl pays a higher
per-DoF setup cost. Table~\ref{tab:viem-vs-dda-cost} summarises the
asymptotic cost and memory for a matrix-free MVP:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Asymptotic cost model. $N$ is the number of unknowns,
$N_g$ the number of AIM or FFT grid points (typically $N_g \sim 2N$
for AIM), $n_{\text{st}}$ the AIM moment matching order, $n_{\text{it}}$
the number of Krylov iterations, and $L$ the number of particle
orientations in a batch solve.}
\label{tab:viem-vs-dda-cost}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l p{45mm}@{}}
\toprule
Quantity & block-DDA\_Py & block-VIEM.jl (half-SWG + AIM) \\
\midrule
Element geometry
  & cubic lattice
  & unstructured tetrahedra \\
Green kernel on grid
  & analytic dyadic, $\mathcal{O}(N_g)$
  & scalar, with moment-matched projection, $\mathcal{O}(N_g)$ \\
Near-field ``precorrection''
  & none (exact on the lattice)
  & Duffy volumetric, $\mathcal{O}(N\,n_{\text{near}})$ \\
MVP cost
  & $\mathcal{O}(N_g \log N_g)$
  & $\mathcal{O}(N_g \log N_g + N\,n_{\text{near}})$ \\
Multi-orientation batch
  & block GMRES on $L$ RHS
  & shared LU (small $N$) or block GMRES on $L$ RHS \\
Dominant memory
  & $\mathcal{O}(N_g)$ FFT kernel
  & $\mathcal{O}(N_g)$ FFT kernel ${}+\mathcal{O}(N\,n_{\text{near}})$ precorr.\ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The central difference is that DDA has a strictly translation-invariant
interaction matrix, so a single FFT on the doubled lattice captures the
entire MVP exactly. VIEM has no such property: each basis function sees
a different discrete support of neighbouring tetrahedra, and the AIM
moment matching is only accurate for pairs whose separation is large
compared with the element size. Near pairs must therefore be computed
directly via Duffy quadrature and stored as a sparse precorrection
matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-precorrection}). For typical settings, this
near-field work dominates both the matrix assembly time and the
per-iteration MVP cost at small to moderate $N$; only for very large
meshes does the $N_g \log N_g$ FFT cost become comparable.

The overall trade-off is therefore: \textbf{VIEM spends more work per
unknown (near-field Duffy integrals and precorrection), but uses an
order of magnitude fewer unknowns to reach a given accuracy for
high-contrast particles with curved boundaries.} For sphere-like,
low-contrast problems DDA's simplicity wins on wall-clock time; for
the high-contrast iron-oxide aggregates and faceted gold nanoparticles
targeted by this project, VIEM is the clear winner — the
$\ell_c = \SI{0.50}{\micro\metre}$ row in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 9.3 (589 DoF, $0.21\%$
$C_{\text{abs}}$ error against the Mie reference) is representative
of production use.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Two-sphere doublet vs MSTM (multi-orientation)}
\label{sec:doublet-mstm}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A second independent cross-validation compares block-VIEM.jl against
the Multi-Sphere T-Matrix package
\textsc{MSTMforCAS.jl}~\cite{MSTMforCAS}, which produces numerically
exact CAS-v2 observables for aggregates of homogeneous spheres.  The
benchmark target is a touching doublet of equal spheres (radius
\SI{0.030}{\micro\metre}, axial gap \SI{0.003}{\micro\metre}) at
$\lambda_0 = \SI{0.638}{\micro\metre}$, three orientations $\beta
\in \{0, \pi/4, \pi/2\}$, and two materials: a high-contrast
dielectric ($m_p = 1.60 + 0.01 i$, polystyrene-like) and plasmonic
gold ($m_p = 0.17525 + 3.4830 i$, Johnson \& Christy 1972).  The
half-SWG VIEM mesh uses $\ell_c = R/5$
($N_{\text{dof}} = 11\,501$) solved by AIM block-BiCGSTAB at
$\text{tol} = 10^{-7}$; the MSTM reference uses VSWF truncation
forced to $N = 15$ (rtol $\le 10^{-6}$ on both materials at
$x \approx 0.30$).  For the dielectric doublet
$|S_{\text{fw,mean}}|$ matches MSTM to $\le 1.6\,\%$ across all
orientations.  For the plasmonic Au doublet the error grows with
$\beta$ from \SI{1.3}{\percent} at $\beta = 0$ to \SI{4.0}{\percent}
at $\beta = \pi/2$, with strong polarisation anisotropy
($\Im S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ at $\beta = \pi/2$ reaches
\SI{10.5}{\percent}) traced to the concentration of the plasmonic
surface mode in the sub-wavelength inter-sphere gap when the
doublet axis is aligned with the incident polarisation.

A four-point mesh-refinement study at $\ell_c = R/k$ for
$k \in \{5, 6, 7, 8\}$, fitted as
$\text{rel.\ err.}(\ell_c) \propto (\ell_c/R)^p$, recovers the
theoretical linear-SWG rate $p \approx 2.10$--$2.49$ on every
component, including the stiff $\Im S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ at
$p = 2.10$.  An earlier two-point fit using only $R/5$ and $R/6$
had given $p \in [0.78, 3.39]$; that spread is now understood as a
two-point artefact.  Phase A (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-surface}) brings
the AIM-operator memory to a near-linear
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{dof}}^{1.02})$ scaling, so the $R/8$
($N_{\text{dof}} = 45\,585$) refinement fits in
\SI{413}{\mebi\byte} of operator memory and is the basis for the
extrapolation that $\ell_c = R/10$ should bring
$\Im S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ below \SI{3}{\percent}.

\paragraph{MSTMforCAS truncation order.} The MSTMforCAS
auto-truncation returns $N_{\text{trunc}} = 3$ at
$x \approx 0.30$, which is converged for the dielectric case but
\emph{under-truncates} the plasmonic Au case --- $|S_{\text{fw,mean}}|$
shifts by $\approx 5\%$ between $N = 3$ and the converged reference
because the inter-sphere coupling amplified by $|m\!\cdot\!x|$
drives higher-order multipoles even though the single-sphere Mie
expansion has long converged.  We therefore force
$N_{\text{trunc}} = 15$ in \texttt{run\_mstm.jl} for both materials,
reaching rtol $\leq 10^{-6}$.  Any new comparison point should
re-confirm convergence with
\texttt{benchmarks/cas\_v2/doublet\_mstm/check\_trunc\_\allowbreak convergence.jl}.

\paragraph{}
The full benchmark setup, the per-orientation Re/Im-resolved error
tables for $S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ and $S_{\text{fw},\varphi}$, the
$\ell_c$-refinement convergence-rate fits, and the Phase A
AIM-operator memory measurements are tabulated in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S\S 7--8.

\section{Spheroid Sweep HDF5 Output}
\label{sec:spheroid-sweep-io}
%======================================================================

For parameter-sweep applications the forward scattering amplitudes on a
five-dimensional grid are written to an HDF5 file in the layout that
\texttt{block-DDA\_Py}~\cite{Moteki2024} produces, so the downstream
consumer \texttt{build\_spheroid\_lut.py} reads VIEM output without
modification. The writer is \texttt{write\_spheroid\_sweep\_h5}
(\texttt{src/spheroid\_sweep\_io.jl}). It follows the
\texttt{pcas\_lut\_schema} contract (version 0.1.0), and the header of
that source file is the reference for the layout summarised here.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parameter grid}
\label{sec:sweep-grid}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The five grid axes are stored at the root level of the file and shared
across wavelength groups:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{log\_D\_ve\_grid} --- base-10 logarithm of the
        volume-equivalent diameter $D_{ve}$ in \si{\micro\metre};
  \item \texttt{RI\_real\_grid} --- real part of the particle refractive
        index, $\Re(m_p)$;
  \item \texttt{log\_AR\_grid} --- base-10 logarithm of
        $\text{bc\_ratio} = b/c$, following the
        \texttt{block-DDA\_Py} convention where $\text{bc\_ratio}>1$ is
        oblate, $<1$ is prolate, and $=1$ is a sphere;
  \item \texttt{cos\_theta\_o\_half\_grid} --- $\cos\theta_o$ on
        $[0,1]$ (half-domain, extended to $[-1,1]$ by the consumer
        using the $\theta_o\to\pi-\theta_o$ mirror symmetry);
  \item \texttt{phi\_o\_grid} --- $\phi_o$ on $[0,\pi]$ (half-domain,
        extended to $[0,2\pi]$ by the consumer using the
        $\phi_o$-periodicity $S(\theta_o,\phi_o+\pi)=S(\theta_o,\phi_o)$).
\end{itemize}
All axes must be equidistant; \texttt{write\_spheroid\_sweep\_h5}
enforces this constraint and raises an error otherwise, because the
downstream \texttt{NdimSpline\_JAX} consumer assumes uniform grids.
For the size axis the stored quantity $\log_{10} D_{ve}$ must be
equidistant, so the sizes are logarithmically spaced.

An optional sixth axis \texttt{psi\_grid} holds the spin angle $\psi$
about the particle $z$ axis. It is written for particles without axial
symmetry (biaxial index or triaxial shape), together with the root
attributes \texttt{axis\_order} and \texttt{psi\_note}. The amplitude
arrays then have $\psi$ as their last dimension.

The root group also carries the attributes \texttt{m\_m},
\texttt{m\_imag}, \texttt{solver\_tol} and
\texttt{block\_viem\_version}, and a group \texttt{provenance} with
the producer repository and version, the git commit and its dirty flag,
the contract version, a hash of the configuration
(\texttt{config\_hash}) and the creation time.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Per-wavelength datasets}
\label{sec:sweep-data}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Results for each wavelength $\lambda_0$ are stored under a group
\texttt{wl\_<value>} (e.g.\ \texttt{wl\_0p638} for
$\lambda_0 = \SI{0.638}{\micro\metre}$), with the attributes
\texttt{wl\_0}, \texttt{m\_m} and \texttt{m\_imag}. The last one is
the imaginary part of the particle refractive index, which is fixed per
wavelength because only the real part is a grid axis. Each group
contains
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{S\_fw\_theta\_re}, \texttt{S\_fw\_theta\_im},
        \texttt{S\_fw\_phi\_re}, \texttt{S\_fw\_phi\_im}: the real and
        imaginary parts of $S_{\text{fw},\theta}$ and
        $S_{\text{fw},\phi}$ on the shape
        $(N_{D_{ve}}, N_{RI}, N_{AR}, N_{u_{1/2}}, N_{\phi_o})$;
  \item \texttt{converged}: a boolean array of shape
        $(N_{D_{ve}}, N_{RI}, N_{AR})$ indicating successful solves.
        Non-converged cells carry \texttt{NaN+NaN$i$} in the four
        amplitude arrays across all $(N_{u_{1/2}},N_{\phi_o})$ entries.
\end{itemize}
The $\phi_o$ axis corresponds exactly to the Euler angle $\alpha$, and
the populating code applies the analytical $\alpha$-expansion
\eqref{eq:alpha-exp-theta}--\eqref{eq:alpha-exp-phi} to the $\alpha = 0$
solves, so only $N_{u_{1/2}}$ independent VIEM solves are required per
$(D_{ve},\Re(m_p),\text{AR})$ triple. With the $\psi$ axis the number
is $N_\psi N_{u_{1/2}}$.

\section{AIM-accelerated block-Krylov multi-orientation solve}
\label{sec:block-krylov}

For large orientation sweeps --- orientation LUTs typically require
$L = 10^2$--$10^3$ incidence directions per $(D_{ve},\Re(m_p),
\text{AR})$ cell --- reusing the same LU factorization of the dense
impedance matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:multi-orient}) becomes infeasible as
soon as $N \gg 10^3$. For those problems \texttt{block-VIEM.jl} v0.2.0
exposes an AIM-accelerated \emph{block-Krylov} path that solves
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:block-system}
  \mathbf{Z}\,\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{X},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times L},
\end{equation}
with a single pre-built AIM operator and a single block iteration that
acts on all $L$ right-hand sides simultaneously.

\paragraph{Block MVP.}
The AIM operator supports the multi-RHS form
\[
  \text{aim\_mvp}(\mathbf{X}) \;=\;
  \tfrac{1}{\epsilon_p}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}
  \;-\;\tfrac{\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}\bigl[
  \mathbf{K}_{\text{AIM}}(\mathbf{X}) + \mathbf{K}_{\text{near}}\mathbf{X}\bigr],
\]
where $\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{K}_{\text{near}}$ are sparse and their
products cost $\mathcal{O}(\text{nnz}\cdot L)$. The half-SWG surface
terms are contained in $\mathbf{K}_{\text{AIM}}$ and
$\mathbf{K}_{\text{near}}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:aim-surface}). The near-field
product and the far-field convolution
$\mathbf{K}_{\text{AIM}}(\mathbf{X})$ are applied column-by-column and
the $L$ columns are dispatched across the available Julia threads via
\texttt{Threads.@threads}; the FFT plans themselves are shared and use
the process-wide FFTW thread pool, so the three levels of parallelism
--- column, FFT, and iteration --- can be balanced as
$n_{\text{col}}\!\times\!n_{\text{FFT}} \approx n_{\text{cores}}$ via
\texttt{BlockVIEM.set\_fft\_threads}. For a single 20-core machine at
$L=8$ we measure a $2.6\times$ per-column speed-up over the serial
baseline.

\paragraph{Block BiCGSTAB.}
The alternative solver (\texttt{method=:aim\_bicgstab}, the default up
to v0.7.0) follows Tadano, Sakurai and Kuramashi~\cite{TSK2009}
and is a direct port of \texttt{block-DDA\_Py}'s
\texttt{bl\_bicgstab\_\allowbreak jacobi\_\allowbreak mvp\_fft},
sans the Jacobi preconditioner
(which is cheap for DDA because the dipole self-term is diagonal, but
would require $\mathcal{O}(N)$ FFT probes for VIEM; the SWG mass matrix
already normalizes the system adequately). Given $\mathbf{B}\in
\mathbb{C}^{N\times L}$, set $\mathbf{X}_0=\mathbf{0}$,
$\mathbf{R}_0=\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{R}_0$, and
$\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_0=\mathbf{R}_0$, then iterate
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{V}_k    &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_k, \\
  \boldsymbol{\alpha}_k
    &= \bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_0^{\!H}\mathbf{V}_k\bigr)^{-1}
       \bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_0^{\!H}\mathbf{R}_k\bigr), \\
  \mathbf{T}_k    &= \mathbf{R}_k - \mathbf{V}_k\boldsymbol{\alpha}_k, \\
  \mathbf{Z}_k    &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}_k, \\
  \xi_k &=
  \frac{\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{Z}_k^{\!H}\mathbf{T}_k\bigr)}
       {\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{Z}_k^{\!H}\mathbf{Z}_k\bigr)}, \\
  \mathbf{X}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{X}_k + \mathbf{P}_k\boldsymbol{\alpha}_k + \xi_k \mathbf{T}_k, \\
  \mathbf{R}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{T}_k - \xi_k \mathbf{Z}_k, \\
  \boldsymbol{\beta}_k
    &= -\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_0^{\!H}\mathbf{V}_k\bigr)^{-1}
       \bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_0^{\!H}\mathbf{Z}_k\bigr), \\
  \mathbf{P}_{k+1} &=
    \mathbf{R}_{k+1} + \bigl(\mathbf{P}_k - \xi_k \mathbf{V}_k\bigr)\boldsymbol{\beta}_k,
\end{align}
stopping once
$\|\mathbf{R}_{k+1}\|_F / \|\mathbf{B}\|_F < \mathrm{tol}$.
Each outer iteration performs exactly two block MVPs
($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$) and four $L\times L$
Gram matrices, so the per-iteration cost is
$\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(T_{\text{MVP}} \cdot L + NL^2\bigr)$. Block BiCGSTAB
can break down when the columns of $\mathbf{R}_k$ lose numerical
independence; in practice this is rare for the physically distinct
incidence directions of a CAS-v2 LUT. It was observed for $L \ge 64$ in
the paper-production pilots, which is why block GMRES is the default.

\paragraph{Block GMRES.}
The default solver (\texttt{method=:aim\_gmres}, since v0.7.1) is
unrestarted Block GMRES~\cite{SimoncSzyld1996}: a
block-Arnoldi recurrence with thin QR factorization of each new block
Krylov vector builds an orthonormal basis
$\mathbf{V}_1,\mathbf{V}_2,\ldots$ of the block Krylov space
$\mathcal{K}_k(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{B})$, together with an upper
block-Hessenberg matrix $\underline{\mathbf{H}}_k$. At iteration $k$
the approximate solution is
$\mathbf{X}_k = \mathbf{X}_0
+ [\mathbf{V}_1|\cdots|\mathbf{V}_k]\mathbf{Y}_k$,
where $\mathbf{Y}_k$ minimizes
$\|\underline{\mathbf{H}}_k\mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{E}_1\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\|_F$
with $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ the thin-QR factor of the initial
residual. The small least-squares problem is updated by an incremental
block-Givens QR factorisation of $\underline{\mathbf{H}}_k$ (since
v0.7.4), so its cost per iteration grows linearly with $k$. The residual
norm decreases monotonically. Unlike BiCGSTAB the memory grows linearly
in the iteration count ($k$ blocks of shape $N\times L$ are
retained).

\paragraph{Numerical verification.}
On an oblate AR$=3$ spheroid
($D_{ve} = 0.40\,\si{\micro\meter}$, $m_p=1.5$,
$\lambda_0=0.638\,\si{\micro\meter}$, $N=2009$ half-SWG DoFs) the two
block solvers reach tolerance $10^{-8}$ in roughly $50$ block
iterations on $L=5$ orientations, and their CAS-v2 forward amplitudes
agree to $\sim 10^{-10}$ relative. Compared to the dense LU
reference, the AIM block-Krylov observables agree to $\sim 0.8\%$,
which is the AIM discretization error at the chosen pitch and not a
Krylov convergence issue. The analytical $+2j\alpha$ spheroid symmetry
(Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha-expansion}) on the AIM block-BiCGSTAB solution holds at
$\sim 5\times 10^{-10}$, confirming that the block iteration preserves
orientation symmetry to the solver tolerance.

\section{Shifted Block GMRES for Refractive-Index Sweeps}
\label{sec:shifted-gmres}
%======================================================================

A lookup table of scattering observables requires the solution of
\eqref{eq:block-system} for many values of the particle refractive
index $m_p$ at a fixed shape, size and wavelength.
For a homogeneous isotropic particle these systems are shifts of one
operator.
This section describes how block-VIEM.jl solves all of them with a
single block Arnoldi process.
The method combines the shifted-Krylov idea that
Ref.~\cite{Argentin2026} applied to the discrete dipole approximation
with the multi-orientation block GMRES of Ref.~\cite{Moteki2026}.
The solver is \texttt{block\_gmres\_shifted}
(\texttt{src/block\_krylov.jl}).
The sweep function is \texttt{solve\_cas\_v2\_orientations\_shifted}
(\texttt{src/shifted\_sweep.jl}).
Both are available from v0.8.0.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Shift structure of the impedance matrix}
\label{sec:shift-structure}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For a homogeneous isotropic particle the impedance matrix is
\eqref{eq:Z-decomposition},
$\mathbf{Z} = \epsilon_p^{-1}\mathbf{M}
 - (\kappa/\epsilon_{\text{bg}})\,\mathbf{K}$.
The mass matrix $\mathbf{M}$ depends on the mesh only.
The radiation matrix $\mathbf{K}$ contains the Green's function at the
background wavenumber $k_0$ and no material constant of the particle.
This holds for the bulk term $K^{A}$ and for the surface terms
$K^{B}$, $K^{C}$ and $K^{D}$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:weakening}, because each of
them carries $\kappa$ as an overall factor.
Define the shift
\begin{equation}
  \tau = \frac{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}{\epsilon_p - \epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
       = \frac{1}{m_r^{2} - 1},
  \qquad m_r = \frac{m_p}{m_m}.
  \label{eq:shift-tau}
\end{equation}
Since $\epsilon_p^{-1} = \kappa\tau/\epsilon_{\text{bg}}$, the
impedance matrix factors as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Z}(m_p)
    = \frac{\kappa}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
      \bigl(\tau\,\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{K}\bigr).
  \label{eq:shift-structure}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side $\mathbf{B}$ of \eqref{eq:block-system} is the
projection of the incident plane waves and does not depend on $m_p$.
A change of refractive index therefore changes only the scalar $\tau$
in front of $\mathbf{M}$ and the scalar factor of the solution.
In the discrete dipole approximation the corresponding shift multiplies
the identity matrix~\cite{Argentin2026}.
Here it multiplies the mass matrix, so one more step is needed to
reach the standard form.

The factorisation \eqref{eq:shift-structure} was checked numerically on
a coarse sphere mesh with half-SWG boundary functions (765 unknowns).
The matrix $\mathbf{K}$ was recovered from $\mathbf{Z}$ assembled for
$\epsilon_p = 2.25 + 0.03i$, $9 + i$ and $-12.1 + 1.22i$.
The three results agree to \num{6.4e-15} in relative Frobenius norm for
the dense matrix and to \num{3.7e-15} for the AIM operator.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Right preconditioning with the mass matrix}
\label{sec:shift-precond}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Let $\mathbf{Y} = (\kappa/\epsilon_{\text{bg}})\,\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}$.
With \eqref{eq:shift-structure} the system
$\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\tau\,\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{C}\bigr)\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{B},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}^{-1},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{X} = \frac{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}{\kappa}\,
               \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}.
  \label{eq:shifted-system}
\end{equation}
The operator $\mathbf{C}$ is the same for all refractive indices.
The residuals of the two systems are equal,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{X}
    = \mathbf{B} - \bigl(\tau\,\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{C}\bigr)\mathbf{Y}.
  \label{eq:shift-residual}
\end{equation}
GMRES applied to \eqref{eq:shifted-system} therefore minimises the
residual of the original system, and the convergence criterion
$\norm{\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{X}}_F / \norm{\mathbf{B}}_F
 < \text{tol}$ of Ref.~\cite{Moteki2026} is kept.
The alternative $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K}$ (left preconditioning)
minimises the residual multiplied by $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ instead.
In the tests of Table~\ref{tab:shift-precond} the two variants needed
the same number of iterations to within two.

The mass matrix is real, sparse, symmetric and positive definite.
Its sparse Cholesky factor is computed once per mesh.
Each application of $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ to a complex block solves for the
real and imaginary parts separately.
For $N = \num{398808}$ unknowns the factorisation took
\SI{12}{\second} and the factor occupies \SI{1.8}{\giga\byte}.
One application to $L = 13$ columns took \SI{1.7}{\second}, against
\SI{8.9}{\second} for one block product with $\mathbf{K}$ (8 cores of
an Intel Xeon E5-2620~v4).
A Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate-gradient solve to a relative residual
of $10^{-13}$ needed about 50 iterations and was 2.5 to 4 times slower.

The preconditioning also reduces the number of iterations of a single
solve.
Table~\ref{tab:shift-precond} compares block GMRES on $\mathbf{Z}$ with
block GMRES on $\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{M}^{-1}$.
The count drops to between 0.23 and 0.59 of the unpreconditioned one.
For the gold sphere with one orientation the unpreconditioned solver
did not reach the tolerance within 200 iterations.
This preconditioner is available for ordinary solves as the option
\texttt{precond = :mass\_right} of
\texttt{solve\_cas\_v2\_orientations}.
The default of that function is unchanged.

\subsection{Block Arnoldi process and the iterate of each shift}
\label{sec:shift-arnoldi}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Let $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{V}_1\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ be the thin QR
factorisation of the right-hand side.
The block Arnoldi process on $\mathbf{C}$ gives, after $k$ steps,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{C}\,\mathcal{V}_k = \mathcal{V}_{k+1}\,\underline{\mathbf{H}}_k,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{V}_k = [\mathbf{V}_1|\cdots|\mathbf{V}_k],
  \label{eq:shift-arnoldi}
\end{equation}
with the $(k+1)L \times kL$ block Hessenberg matrix
$\underline{\mathbf{H}}_k$.
The block Krylov space of $\tau\,\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{C}$ from
$\mathbf{B}$ does not depend on $\tau$, and
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\tau\,\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{C}\bigr)\mathcal{V}_k
    = \mathcal{V}_{k+1}
      \bigl(\tau\,\underline{\mathbf{I}}_k - \underline{\mathbf{H}}_k\bigr),
  \label{eq:shift-invariance}
\end{equation}
where $\underline{\mathbf{I}}_k$ is the $kL \times kL$ identity matrix
with $L$ zero rows appended.
One basis therefore serves all shifts.
The GMRES iterate of shift $\tau_s$ is
$\mathbf{Y}_s = \mathcal{V}_k\,\mathbf{y}_s$ with
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y}_s
    = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{y}}
      \bigl\|\mathbf{E}_1\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
        - \bigl(\tau_s\,\underline{\mathbf{I}}_k
                - \underline{\mathbf{H}}_k\bigr)\mathbf{y}\bigr\|_F .
  \label{eq:shift-lsq}
\end{equation}
Each block iteration costs one block product with $\mathbf{C}$,
whatever the number of shifts $N_m$.

The small problems \eqref{eq:shift-lsq} are handled by an incremental
block-Givens QR factorisation, one per shift, as in
\texttt{block\_gmres}.
A new block column of $\tau_s\,\underline{\mathbf{I}}_k
- \underline{\mathbf{H}}_k$ is first transformed by the stored
$2L \times 2L$ rotations of that shift.
A QR factorisation of its last $2L \times L$ block gives the next
rotation.
The Frobenius norm of the last block of the transformed right-hand
side is the residual norm of the shift.
A shift is frozen at the first iteration where its relative residual
is below the tolerance.
The process stops when all shifts are frozen or when the iteration cap
is reached.
Shifts that have not converged by then are reported as such, with
their minimum-residual iterate.

The triangular factor of a shift is not stored during the iteration.
It is rebuilt from the shared Hessenberg columns and the rotations of
the shift when the shift is frozen.
The memory per shift is then $k$ rotations of $4L^{2}$ complex numbers
each, instead of a triangular matrix of order $kL$.
The basis takes $16\,N L (k+1)$ bytes as in the unshifted solver.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Radiation operator from the AIM operator}
\label{sec:shift-k0}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The AIM operator of Sec.~\ref{sec:aim} is built once, for the
refractive index of largest $\abs{\kappa}$ in the list.
Its far-field part and its near-field precorrection both store
$\kappa\,\mathbf{K}$.
The function \texttt{aim\_K0\_mvp} applies these two parts and divides
by $\kappa$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{K}\mathbf{X}
    = \frac{1}{\kappa_{\text{ref}}}
      \bigl[\mathbf{K}_{\text{AIM}}(\mathbf{X})
            + \mathbf{K}_{\text{near}}\mathbf{X}\bigr].
  \label{eq:shift-k0}
\end{equation}
No subtraction of the mass term is involved, so there is no loss of
digits.
The operator build is the dominant set-up cost of the VIEM
(Sec.~\ref{sec:shift-cost}), and it is done once per mesh instead of
once per refractive index.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Solutions and observables}
\label{sec:shift-observables}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

After the Arnoldi process the solutions are formed one refractive index
at a time,
$\mathbf{X}_s = (\epsilon_{\text{bg}}/\kappa_s)\,
 \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{k_s}\mathbf{y}_s$,
and passed to the existing observable routines of
Sec.~\ref{sec:observables} with $\epsilon_p = \epsilon_{p,s}$.
The definitions of the observables are untouched, and only one
solution block is held in memory at a time.
As a guard against loss of orthogonality in a long Arnoldi process the
residual $\norm{\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{Z}_s\mathbf{X}_s}_F /
\norm{\mathbf{B}}_F$ can be recomputed from $\mathbf{M}$ and
$\mathbf{K}$ after the solve.
By default this is done for the refractive index with the most
iterations, at the cost of one block product.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:shift-scope}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
  \item The particle must be homogeneous and isotropic.
        For an anisotropic permittivity (Sec.~\ref{sec:anisotropic}) the
        contrast differs between the Cartesian components and the
        change of refractive index is not a scalar shift.
        The sweep function rejects such input.
  \item All refractive indices share one mesh and one AIM grid.
        The mesh must resolve the largest $\abs{m_p}$ of the list.
  \item The number of block iterations is set by the slowest
        refractive index of the list.
        Indices of similar contrast should be solved together.
        Convergence is slow when $\tau$ lies close to the real interval
        $[-1, 0]$, which is the case for metals
        ($\tau \approx -0.076 - 0.007i$ for gold at
        \SI{638}{\nano\meter}).
  \item The solver is unrestarted GMRES.
        Block BiCGSTAB has no shifted counterpart here.
\end{itemize}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:shift-cost}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider $N_m$ refractive indices on one mesh with $L$ orientations.
Let $T_{\text{sh}}$ be the time of the mesh generation, the AIM
projection and the mass matrix, $t_{\text{op}}$ the time to build the
AIM operator (Green's function FFT and near-field precorrection),
$t_{\text{mvp}}$ the time of one block product and $t_{\text{post}}$
the time to evaluate the observables of one refractive index.
One solve per refractive index costs
\begin{equation}
  T_{\text{B}}
    = T_{\text{sh}}
      + \sum_{s=1}^{N_m}
        \bigl(t_{\text{op}} + n_s^{\mathbf{Z}}\,t_{\text{mvp}}
              + t_{\text{post}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:shift-cost-B}
\end{equation}
where $n_s^{\mathbf{Z}}$ is the number of block-GMRES iterations on
$\mathbf{Z}_s$.
The shifted sweep costs
\begin{equation}
  T_{\text{C}}
    = T_{\text{sh}} + t_{\text{op}} + t_{\text{chol}}
      + \max_s n_s\,\bigl(t_{\text{mvp}} + t_{\mathbf{M}^{-1}}\bigr)
      + N_m\,\bigl(t_{\mathbf{M}^{-1}} + t_{\text{post}}\bigr)
      + t_{\text{small}},
  \label{eq:shift-cost-C}
\end{equation}
where $n_s$ is the number of iterations of shift $s$ on
$\mathbf{C}$, $t_{\text{chol}}$ the Cholesky factorisation time,
$t_{\mathbf{M}^{-1}}$ the time of one block solve with the factor and
$t_{\text{small}}$ the time spent on orthogonalisation and on the
small problems \eqref{eq:shift-lsq}.

Three effects contribute to $T_{\text{B}}/T_{\text{C}}$.
The operator is built once instead of $N_m$ times.
In our measurements at $L = 13$ this build costs as much as 70 to 280
block products, so
it is the largest term of \eqref{eq:shift-cost-B} for small and
medium meshes.
The preconditioning lowers the iteration count of every refractive
index (Table~\ref{tab:shift-precond}).
The shifts share one Krylov space, so the products are paid for the
slowest shift only.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Use in the parameter sweeps}
\label{sec:shift-sweeps}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{sloppypar}
The sweep drivers
\nolinkurl{benchmarks/cas_v2/ironoxide_sweep_h5.jl} and
\nolinkurl{benchmarks/cas_v2/spheroid_sweep_h5.jl} accept the option
\texttt{--shifted-ri}.
All refractive indices of a (wavelength, $D_{ve}$, aspect ratio) row
are then solved by one call of
\texttt{solve\_cas\_v2\_orientations\_shifted}.
Both drivers already share the mesh across the refractive-index axis
in their default mode.
The option therefore changes neither the mesh nor the AIM grid nor the
convergence criterion, and the stored amplitudes agree with the default
mode to the solver tolerance.
This differs from block-DDA\_Py, where the shifted mode replaces the
per-index lattice by a common one and the values change at the level
of the discretisation error.
The option is allowed for the triaxial shape mode, which keeps the
index isotropic.
It is rejected for the anisotropic index modes.
A file written with the option carries the root attribute
\texttt{shifted\_ri}, which also enters the configuration hash.
The output of the default mode is unchanged.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
The general sweep driver \nolinkurl{viem_results/run_viem.jl} accepts
the same option.
All isotropic entries of the refractive-index list of a (shape,
wavelength) slot are solved together on the per-shape mesh of the
default mode.
Anisotropic entries are solved one at a time as before.
In the cost group of the output file the time fields of the entries
solved together hold the time of the batch divided by the number of
entries, and the iteration count, the convergence flag and the residual
are those of the individual refractive index.
This corresponds to \texttt{run\_dda.py --shifted-ri} of block-DDA\_Py.
\end{sloppypar}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:shift-verification}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The tests are in \texttt{test/test\_shifted\_krylov.jl} and
\texttt{test/test\_shifted\_sweep.jl}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Solver.}
        For a random dense $\mathbf{C}$ ($N = 200$, $L = 4$, five
        shifts) every solution agrees with a direct solve to better
        than $10^{-10}$.
        For one shift the iteration count and the residual history
        equal those of \texttt{block\_gmres} applied to
        $\tau\,\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{C}$.
  \item \emph{Formulation against dense direct solves.}
        On the coarse sphere mesh the matrix $\mathbf{K}$ is taken from
        one dense $\mathbf{Z}$ and the shifted solver is run for
        $m_p = 1.5 + 0.01i$, $1.75$, $2.0$ and $2.5 + 0.2i$.
        The solutions agree with LU solves of separately assembled
        matrices to better than $10^{-7}$ and the CAS-v2 amplitudes to
        better than $10^{-6}$ at $\text{tol} = 10^{-10}$.
  \item \emph{Sweep function against the existing AIM path.}
        With the same AIM grid and $\text{tol} = 10^{-8}$ the amplitudes
        of the shifted sweep agree with those of one
        \texttt{:aim\_gmres} solve per refractive index to better than
        $10^{-6}$.
        The errors against Mie theory are the same for both.
  \item \emph{Residual.}
        The recomputed residual
        $\norm{\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{Z}_s\mathbf{X}_s}_F /
        \norm{\mathbf{B}}_F$ is below the tolerance for every
        refractive index and equals the residual reported by the solver.
  \item \emph{Sweep drivers.}
        For a small iron-oxide sweep at $\text{tol} = 10^{-8}$ the
        output of \texttt{--shifted-ri} agrees with that of the default
        mode to \num{3.9e-10} relative to the largest amplitude of the
        wavelength group (\num{2.8e-10} with the triaxial shape).
        The default mode writes the same numbers, attributes and
        configuration hash as before the change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Measured speed-up}
\label{sec:shift-benchmark}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{sloppypar}
The script \nolinkurl{benchmarks/shifted/run_shift_scaling.jl} times
three ways of solving a refractive-index sweep on one mesh.
Method~B calls the existing block GMRES once per refractive index.
The mesh, the AIM projection and the mass matrix are shared and the
AIM operator is rebuilt for every index.
This is the default mode of the iron-oxide sweep driver.
Method~P is method~B with right preconditioning by the mass matrix.
Method~C is the shifted block GMRES, which is the mode
\texttt{--shifted-ri}.
All three solve the same linear systems on the same mesh and AIM grid.
\end{sloppypar}

The test particle is an oblate spheroid with $b/c = 2$ in vacuum at
$\lambda_0 = \SI{0.638}{\micro\meter}$.

from $2.7 + 0.1i$ to $3.1 + 0.1i$ (iron oxide) and from $1.35$ to
$1.70$ (dielectric, the range of the block-DDA\_Py benchmark).
For $N_m = 1$ the single index is the upper end.
The orientations have $\alpha = \gamma = 0$ and $\cos\beta$ equispaced

\subsection{Comparison with block-DDA\_Py}
\label{sec:shift-vs-dda}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The same method is implemented in block-DDA\_Py (v0.8.0 to v0.8.3).
The two codes differ in three points.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the DDA the shift multiplies the identity matrix and no
        preconditioner is needed.
        In the VIEM it multiplies the mass matrix, and a sparse
        Cholesky solve is added to every block product.
  \item In the DDA the set-up per refractive index is cheap, and the
        gain comes from the shared Krylov space.
        The measured gain of the DDA sweep over its default mode is
        7.7 to 9.0 for $N_m = 15$ and 15 to 18 for $N_m = 30$.
        In the VIEM the near-field precorrection dominates the set-up,
        and building it once gives a gain close to $N_m$.
  \item In the DDA the shifted mode replaces the per-index lattice by a
        common one, so the stored values change at the level of the
        discretisation error.
        In the VIEM sweep drivers the mesh was already common, and the
        stored values change at the level of the solver tolerance only.
\end{itemize}

\section{Particle shape models and cross-validation}
\label{sec:gre}

The two particle-shape families supported by \texttt{block-VIEM.jl}
are the Gaussian Random Ellipsoid (GRE) parameterised by
$(r_{v,\text{base}},\; b\!/\!c,\; a\!/\!b,\; \beta)$ following
Muinonen \& Pieniluoma~\cite{MuinonenPieniluoma2011}, and the
sphere-aggregate model represented by a \texttt{SphereAggregate}
container of monomer centres and radii.  Both pipelines emit a
single conforming tetrahedral mesh, share the same volume-preserving
final rescale (so the discretised volume matches the analytical
target to machine precision, in parity with block-DDA\_Py's
\texttt{Target.\_\_init\_\_} spacing adjustment), and feed the SWG /
half-SWG basis of Sec.~\ref{sec:swg}.  For an aggregate of monomers
separated by gaps the rescale is applied to each disconnected piece
about the centre of its own monomers (from v0.8.2), so the centre
distances keep their nominal values on any mesh.  The full specification of
parameter ranges, the \texttt{neck\_ratio} neck-width convention,
the equal-radius polydisperse extension, the adaptive characteristic
length, and worked discretisation examples is given in
\texttt{docs/descriptions\_particle\_shape\_model.md} and is not
reproduced here.

Cross-validation against block-DDA\_Py is performed on three
deformed GRE configurations with $\beta = 0.10, 0.15, 0.20$ and
\textsc{bc\_ratio}/\textsc{ab\_ratio} in the range
$[1.0, 3.0]$ at $\lambda_0 = \SI{0.638}{\micro\meter}$, $m_p = 1.5
+ 0.01\,i$, $r_{v,\text{base}} = \SI{0.20}{\micro\meter}$, two
tilt angles $\beta_{\text{ori}} = \pi/4, \pi/2$.  The
\emph{same} Gaussian random deformation field $s(\theta,\phi)$ is
generated by the Python driver and consumed by the Julia driver via
\texttt{gre\_mesh\_with\_field} so both codes solve the identical
shape.  Relative errors on the complex forward amplitudes are
0.1--2.6\,\%, consistent with the discretisation-error level
observed in the smooth-spheroid benchmark
(Sec.~\ref{sec:viem-vs-dda}); per-case tables are in
\texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 5.

\section{Anisotropic Permittivity}
\label{sec:anisotropic}

Version 0.4.0 extends the EFVIE-D formulation to diagonal-tensor
permittivity
$\bar{\epsilon}_p = \operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_x, \epsilon_y, \epsilon_z)$
in the particle frame, matching the \texttt{m\_p\_xyz} parameter of
block-DDA\_Py.

\subsection{Modified weak form}

Defining the component-wise contrast
$\kappa_\alpha = (\epsilon_\alpha - \epsilon_{\text{bg}})/\epsilon_\alpha$
and its average
$\bar{\kappa} = (\kappa_x + \kappa_y + \kappa_z)/3$,
the Galerkin impedance element becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aniso-zmn}
  Z_{mn}
  = \sum_\alpha \frac{1}{\epsilon_\alpha}
    \int (f_m)_\alpha\,(f_n)_\alpha\,dV
  - \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
    \iint \!\Bigl[
      k_0^2 \sum_\alpha \kappa_\alpha\,(f_m)_\alpha\,(f_n)_\alpha
      \;-\; \bar{\kappa}\,(\nabla\!\cdot\!\bm{f}_m)\,
             (\nabla'\!\cdot\!\bm{f}_n)
    \Bigr] G\,dV'\,dV.
\end{equation}
The simplification $\nabla'\!\cdot(\bar{\kappa}\cdot\bm{f}_n)
= \bar{\kappa}\,\nabla'\!\cdot\bm{f}_n$ exploits the SWG structure
$\partial(f_n)_\alpha/\partial r_\alpha = a_n/(3V)$ (identical for all
$\alpha$).

\subsection{Implementation}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mass term:} component-weighted inner product
  $\sum_\alpha (1/\epsilon_\alpha)\int (f_m)_\alpha(f_n)_\alpha\,dV$,
  in the dense assembly and in the AIM operator (sparse matrix
  \texttt{mass\_weighted}). Up to v0.8.0 the AIM operator used
  $\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha^{-1}$ times the unweighted
  mass matrix instead. This changed the observables of anisotropic
  particles by \SIrange{5}{25}{\percent} in the cases tested, so
  anisotropic results of the AIM path from those versions are not
  reliable. The dense path was not affected.
\item \textbf{Radiation kernel (bulk):} $k_0^2$ term uses per-component
  $\kappa_\alpha$; $\nabla\nabla\!\cdot$ term uses $\bar{\kappa}$.
\item \textbf{Half-SWG surface terms} ($K^B$, $K^C$, $K^D$): scaled by
  $\bar{\kappa}$ (exact for isotropic; $O(|\Delta\kappa|/|\kappa|)$
  approximation for anisotropic).
\item \textbf{AIM FFT-MVP:} per-channel $\kappa_\alpha$ weighting on
  the $x,y,z$ vector channels; $\bar{\kappa}$ on the divergence channel.
\item \textbf{Far-field amplitude:}
  $\bm{F} = (k_0^2/\epsilon_{\text{bg}})
  (\mathbf{I} - \hat{\bm{r}}\hat{\bm{r}})\cdot
  (\kappa_x P_x,\ \kappa_y P_y,\ \kappa_z P_z)^{\top}$,
  with the component-wise contrasts and not their average.
\item \textbf{Absorption:}
  $C_{\text{abs}} = \frac{k_0}{\epsilon_{\text{bg}}}
  \sum_\alpha \frac{\operatorname{Im}(\epsilon_\alpha)}{|\epsilon_\alpha|^2}
  \int |D_\alpha|^2\,dV$.
\end{itemize}
When $\epsilon_x = \epsilon_y = \epsilon_z$ all expressions reduce to the
isotropic formulation of Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation} and the following
sections.

\subsection{Cross-validation vs block-DDA\_Py}
\label{sec:aniso-crossval}

Cross-validation against block-DDA\_Py is performed on a sphere
($r_{ve} = \SI{0.20}{\micro\meter}$,
$\lambda_0 = \SI{0.638}{\micro\meter}$, $n_m = 1$,
$\ell_c = \SI{0.035}{\micro\meter}$, $N_{\text{VIEM}} \approx 8000$
half-SWG DoFs) with three diagonal-tensor configurations
$\bm m_p = (m_x, m_y, m_z)$ ranging from isotropic
$[1.5,1.5,1.5]$ to strongly anisotropic $[1.6,1.5,1.4]$, at tilt
angles $\beta_{\text{ori}} = \pi/4, \pi/2$.  The isotropic baseline
matches the existing spheroid benchmark accuracy
(${\sim}\,1.3$--$2.4\,\%$); errors grow moderately with anisotropy
strength, reaching ${\sim}\,5\%$ for the ``strong'' case at
$\beta_{\text{ori}} = \pi/2$, dominated by the $\bar{\kappa}$
approximation in the half-SWG surface terms and the
cubic-vs-tetrahedral discretisation mismatch.  Per-case tables are
in \texttt{docs/benchmark\_results.md} \S 6.
